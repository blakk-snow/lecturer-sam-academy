## How to use this answer book

Grammar, vocabulary and comprehension answers are given in full. Writing and speaking tasks cannot have single answers, so they carry marking guidance instead: what a strong response contains, and what to listen or look for.

Where more than one answer is acceptable the note says so. In language work this is the rule rather than the exception, and a learner who has produced something other than the answer printed here has not necessarily made a mistake.

The marking notes at the end of each chapter name the errors to expect. They are drawn from the misconceptions set out in the Learner's Book.

*Strand 1: Oral Language (Listening and Speaking)*

### Chapter 1: Speaking in Everyday Situations

#### Recall

**1.** Register.

**2.** Please may I have that form?

**3.** Any question beginning with why, how or what.

**4.** Any two of: a church, a mosque, a school, a clinic, a filling station.

**5.** Informal.

**6.** cannot

**7.** For example: stale, sweet, sour, smoky.

**8.** Turn taking.

#### Key words

**1.** reservation

**2.** courtesy

**3.** elaboration

**4.** sense or sensory

**5.** complex

**6.** turn taking

**7.** yielding

**8.** It adds nothing new. A contribution supplies evidence, a counter-case, a distinction or a consequence — something the next speaker can take hold of.

#### Core practice

**1.** The first formal with a greeting; the second informal but courteous.

**2.** Greet and identify; state the business; give the details; confirm and close.

**3.** Named occasion, full details, and a courteous request.

**4.** For example: What would make farming profitable for a school leaver? In what way is the internet risky for a young child?

**5.** Three different kinds, each adding something.

**6.** For example: a sweeping view; a stale smell; a dense crowd.

**7.** Any sentence using smell, sound, taste or touch.

**8.** Three correct uses in directions.

**9.** Taking: *May I come in here? If I may add something…* Yielding: *That is all from me. What do you think, Ama?*

**10.** Overlapping speech is normal among friends and shows interest. In a meeting the chairperson allocates turns, and interrupting takes the floor away from somebody.

#### Challenge

**1.** All four moves visible in the dialogue.

**2.** All the required features present and marked.

**3.** Order, permanent landmarks, distances, warning.

**4.** The concession must be genuine and the argument must continue.

<!-- page 4 -->

**5.** A chairperson allocates the floor and brings in those who have not spoken; unequal turns mean the meeting has heard fewer views than it might.

#### Exam-style

**1.** The occasion sets the least formality allowed; the partner decides how far above it you stand. Two contrasting examples required. **[4]**

**2.** Greet and identify, state the business, give the details, confirm and close. **[3]**

**3.** Any versions requiring explanation. **[3]**

**4.** Evidence, counter-case, distinction, consequence, with one example. **[4]**

**5.** Order, landmarks, distance. **[3]**

**6.** Speak when recognised by the chair; yield by finishing cleanly or by putting a question to another speaker. **[3]**

#### Writing task — marking guidance

Ten marks: full details of the occasion in both versions (3), a clear statement of what the guest must do (2), courtesy rather than command (2), three differences underlined (2), the spoken version sounds like speech (1). Learners commonly write the spoken version as a second letter; the differences underlined are what expose this.

#### Speaking and listening — what to look for

- **The two errands.** The class should name features — greeting, complete sentences, absence of contractions — rather than say that one *sounded better*.

- **The round table.** The tally is the assessment. An even tally with weak contributions is better than a brilliant monologue.

- Sign the log only after the task has been performed aloud.

#### Marking notes and common errors

- **Contributions, not agreements.** Most learners think agreeing is participating. The four kinds in Guided Practice 2 give them something to do instead.

- **Permanent landmarks.** A parked lorry is not a landmark. Ask of each: will it be there next year?

- **Speaking to somebody who disagrees** is not the same as speaking to somebody who does not yet know. It is worth naming this distinction aloud.

- **Core 1 and Challenge 1** work best performed rather than written; use the writing as the script.

### Chapter 2: Listening for Key Information

#### Recall

**1.** The message.

**2.** The mood.

**3.** The tone.

**4.** F

**5.** govt, no., ↑, ∴ — any one.

**6.** Eighty-two thousand.

**7.** Suggested but not stated.

**8.** A figure.

#### Key words

**1.** discussion

**2.** talk

**3.** bulletin

**4.** explicit

**5.** loaded

**6.** understatement

**7.** concession

**8.** Several threads run at once and they cross. The listener must sort as well as understand, keeping track of who said what.

#### Core practice

**1.** Marked on the sorting rather than on the content.

**2.** Both supported by what was actually said.

**3.** (a) sarcastic (b) restrained or resentful (c) impatient

**4.** (a) that his age would normally excuse laziness — *for someone his age* (b) that the number is small and unimportant — *only*

**5.** crowd, thrifty, plan, government

**6.** Who, what, when and where, how many, why, so what.

**7.** Dir: 82,000 doses received.

**8.** (a) F (b) O (c) F

**9.** For example: I take the point that the harvest made it difficult, but the harvest comes every year.

**10.** Tone is the speaker's attitude, not the volume. A speaker can be furious in a whisper.

#### Challenge

**1.** The material below the line is the assessment; expect implied meaning rather than facts.

**2.** The difference must lie in word choice alone.

**3.** That the speaker has reservations they will not state — the guarded wording and the refusal.

**4.** Marked on the accuracy of the figures and on the checking.

**5.** Agreement with everybody means the differences between speakers have not been noticed, and those differences are where the thinking is.

<!-- page 6 -->

#### Exam-style

**1.** Message: what you are meant to take away. Mood: what you feel. Tone: the speaker's attitude to the subject. **[4]**

**2.** Sarcastic — *naturally*; grudging — *managed*. **[4]**

**3.** crowd, thrifty, government **[3]**

**4.** Who, what, when and where, how many, why, so what. **[3]**

**5.** Admitting part of the other case; the sentence must do both. **[3]**

**6.** The speaker is minimising the problem; *a few* makes it sound smaller than it may be. **[3]**

#### Writing task — marking guidance

Ten marks: claims attributed correctly (3), agreement and disagreement both identified (2), any change of position noted (1), the writer's own view kept separate and labelled (2), the final paragraph opening with a genuine concession (2). A report that treats the discussion as one voice has missed the point of the chapter.

#### Speaking and listening — what to look for

- **The one-minute report.** Listen for named speakers. A learner who says *they said* has not sorted the discussion.

- **The group discussion.** The inference must come with its words attached; challenge any that does not.

- Sign the log only after the task has been performed aloud.

#### Marking notes and common errors

- **Tone is attitude.** Repeat as often as necessary; it is the single most common error in listening papers.

- **Loaded words.** The neutral-partner exercise in Core 5 is the quickest way to make learners hear them.

- **Concessions.** A learner who can concede before disagreeing will do better in the Basic 9 debate chapter than one who cannot.

- **Core 1, 2 and Challenge 1** need a recording or a reader.

### Chapter 3: English Consonant Sounds

#### Recall

**1.** a, e, i, o, u

**2.** Voiceless.

**3.** think, three, thumb — any one.

**4.** Two words differing in one sound only.

**5.** bed, road — any two.

**6.** The vowel in *sheep*.

**7.** The past tense.

**8.** The plural.

#### Key words

**1.** consonant

**2.** voicing

**3.** plosive

**4.** fricative

**5.** nasal

**6.** affricate

**7.** cluster

**8.** Put two fingers on your throat and make the sound. If it buzzes under your fingers it is voiced; if it is silent it is voiceless.

#### Core practice

**1.** Voiceless: p, f, s, k. Voiced: b, v, z, g.

**2.** plosive, fricative, nasal, affricate

**3.** bay, den, goat, bag

**4.** tink is not a word — accept *thin/tin*; day, prize, cheap

**5.** think, three, both; this, mother, breathe

**6.** str, skt, lmz, rld

**7.** past tense, plural, possession, third person singular

**8.** Marked by ear on the endings.

**9.** /ʃ/

**10.** sing, thing, long — any two.

#### Challenge

**1.** /ʃ/ can be sustained; /tʃ/ happens once and is over. For example *catch* and *cash*.

**2.** The /t/ carries the past tense. Without it the sentence loses its time, and the listener hears a present-tense verb with a past-tense adverb.

**3.** Ten correct pairs and a recorded score.

**4.** It is a regular feature of a variety spoken by millions. The contrast is worth having available for oral examinations, dictations and listeners unfamiliar with the accent.

**5.** Any workable twister in which the contrast genuinely recurs.

<!-- page 8 -->

#### Exam-style

**1.** Where the air is obstructed, how, and whether the voice is on. /v/: top teeth on bottom lip, fricative, voiced. **[4]**

**2.** Voiceless p, s; voiced b, z. **[2]**

**3.** bay, goat, prize **[3]**

**4.** Tongue tip between the teeth, air pushed through; voice off for /θ/, on for /ð/. **[4]**

**5.** /skt/; the past tense. **[3]**

**6.** The dropped consonant carries the tense, so the sentence loses its time reference. **[4]**

#### Writing task — marking guidance

Ten marks: length (1), eight final clusters used and underlined (3), six grammatical endings used and double- underlined (3), the passage makes sense (1), an honest note on the writer's own difficulties (2). Mark the underlining by sound, not by spelling.

#### Speaking and listening — what to look for

- **The dictation.** Back to back, so that lips cannot be read. Record both scores and note the pairs the class as a whole found hardest.

- **The reading.** Mark the endings, not the accent. Do not deduct for Ghanaian pronunciation; deduct only where a grammatical ending has disappeared.

- Sign the log only after the task has been performed aloud.

#### Marking notes and common errors

- **Voicing is felt, not heard.** The fingers on the throat settle every dispute in this chapter.

- **Grammatical endings** are the practical heart of the work: /t/, /d/, /s/, /z/ at the ends of words carry tense, number and possession.

- **Respect.** The Ghanaian English material in Guided Practice 2 and Challenge 4 is deliberate and should be taught as written.

- **Core 4** contains a deliberate trap: *tink* is not an English word, so the pair is *thin* and *tin*.

*Strand 2: Reading*

### Chapter 4: Reading Widely and Independently

#### Recall

**1.** Non-fiction.

**2.** Searching a text for one particular piece of information.

**3.** Reading quickly to get the general sense.

**4.** A heading, a caption, a table, bold print — any one.

**5.** The central or main idea.

**6.** Use the context around it.

**7.** Any reasoned prediction about mobile money or banking.

**8.** What you intend to get out of the text.

#### Key words

**1.** non-fiction

**2.** monitoring

**3.** fix-up

**4.** visualisation

**5.** prior

**6.** right there

**7.** author and you

**8.** Knowing what you want tells you what to skip. A reader without a purpose reads everything at the same speed and remembers less of it.

#### Core practice

**1.** Held together by ideas rather than events; you track claims and evidence; help is in headings and captions; you may skip what is not on your purpose.

**2.** Two purposes, each naming what is wanted.

**3.** A blank page, an unattached pronoun, a sentence that does not follow, a wandering mind.

**4.** Any two correct pairings.

**5.** One line per paragraph.

**6.** Three predictions honestly marked.

**7.** (a) right there (b) think and search (c) author and you (d) on your own

**8.** Four questions, correctly labelled.

**9.** Both sources named.

**10.** Any description showing the reader has seen it.

#### Challenge

**1.** The question asks for a position with reasons drawn from the text and from experience; the model answer must supply both.

**2.** Marked on whether the *confused* column has anything in it.

**3.** The rewrite must add concrete detail, not merely simplify.

**4.** Weak readers plough on because going back feels like failure. Strong readers go back the moment the thread breaks and finish with more.

**5.** Marked on completeness and honesty.

<!-- page 11 -->

#### Exam-style

**1.** Any three correct differences. **[3]**

**2.** Right there, think and search, author and you, on your own — each with its location. **[4]**

**3.** Four signals and two correct strategies. **[4]**

**4.** It tells the reader what to skip. **[3]**

**5.** Turning words into pictures; readers do it naturally with stories and rarely with information, which is why information is harder to remember. **[3]**

**6.** A position, at least one reason, and a link to something in the text or in experience. **[3]**

#### Writing task — marking guidance

Ten marks: purpose written before reading (2), predictions marked honestly (2), one line per paragraph (3), the fix- up strategy named rather than described vaguely (2), a genuine unanswered question (1).

#### Speaking and listening — what to look for

- **Thinking aloud.** An empty *confused* column means the learner has stopped monitoring, not that the article was easy.

- **The group discussion.** Insist on all three kinds of contribution; learners default to opinion.

- Sign the log only after the task has been performed aloud.

#### Marking notes and common errors

- **Question types.** Learners who know the four types stop hunting for a stated answer to an inference question, which is where most comprehension time is lost.

- **Purpose before reading**, every time. Written afterwards it teaches nothing.

- **Re-reading.** Say explicitly that going back is what good readers do; many learners believe the opposite.

- **Challenge 5** runs over two weeks.

### Chapter 5: Comprehending and Interpreting Texts

#### Recall

**1.** The byline.

**2.** The topic sentence.

**3.** However (or but, whereas).

**4.** Removing or reversing.

**5.** Implicit meaning.

**6.** Textual evidence.

**7.** government

**8.** An idea about life that a text carries beyond its own subject.

#### Key words

**1.** feature

**2.** dateline

**3.** pull

**4.** cohesive

**5.** contextual

**6.** derivation

**7.** viewpoint

**8.** Every writer chooses what to include. What is missing shapes the reader's impression as much as what is present, and often shows what the writer did not want to complicate.

#### Core practice

**1.** Title, byline, dateline, subheadings, captions, tables, pull quotes, sidebars — any six with correct uses.

**2.** Definition, example, contrast, cause and effect, restatement.

**3.** (a) erosion: the wearing away of soil (definition) (b) taciturn: not talkative (contrast)

**4.** Contrast, result, example, concession.

**5.** Who wrote it, when, for whom, and in what place or culture.

**6.** Three claims, each with a quotation.

**7.** A sentence about life, plus an application.

**8.** transport, import, export, portable; predict, dictate, dictionary, contradict

**9.** government, governmental, govern; industry, industrial, industrialise

**10.** *Industrial* relates to factories and industry; *industrious* means hard-working.

#### Challenge

**1.** Marked on completeness and on the contributions named.

**2.** The difference must be real, not a restatement.

**3.** The strong answer states the interpretation, quotes exactly, and names the word doing the work.

**4.** The missing voices must be genuinely absent from the text.

**5.** Four correct sentences.

#### Exam-style

**1.** Any six with correct uses. **[4]**

<!-- page 13 -->

**2.** Definition, example, contrast, cause and effect, restatement. **[3]**

**3.** Contrast, result, example. **[3]**

**4.** Without them it is an opinion about the text rather than a reading of it. **[3]**

**5.** A sentence about life plus a genuine application. **[4]**

**6.** Four words plus the correct distinction. **[3]**

#### Writing task — marking guidance

Ten marks: writer, date and reader identified (2), text features listed with contributions (2), two implications each with exact words (3), theme stated as a sentence (2), a missing voice named (1). An analysis that summarises the article instead of analysing it earns the first two marks only.

#### Speaking and listening — what to look for

- **The two-minute analysis.** Listen for the quoted words. A learner who says the article *seems biased* without quoting has not done the work.

- **The role-play.** The missing party is the point of the exercise; make sure somebody plays them.

- Sign the log only after the task has been performed aloud.

#### Marking notes and common errors

- **Read the features first.** Ninety seconds on title, byline, dateline and subheadings changes how the whole article is read.

- **Evidence.** An interpretation without quoted words scores nothing at this level, and the requirement grows stricter in Basic 9.

- **Themes as sentences.** *Responsibility* is a topic. *Damage that belongs to everybody is repaired by nobody* is a theme.

- **Derivation** grows vocabulary faster than word lists. Keep a root wall in the classroom.

### Chapter 6: Citing Evidence and Summarising

#### Recall

**1.** The topic sentence.

**2.** Cash crops.

**3.** gift

**4.** Quotation marks.

**5.** Containing no opinion of the summariser.

**6.** now

**7.** Six.

**8.** A restatement in your own words at similar length.

#### Key words

**1.** central

**2.** implied

**3.** illustration

**4.** aside

**5.** redundancy

**6.** proportions

**7.** citation

**8.** An assertion claims something about a text. An analysis claims it and shows the words that prove it, naming which word does the work.

#### Core practice

**1.** Three sentences, each covering its whole paragraph.

**2.** The method must be that every sentence pointed one way.

**3.** Reasons and evidence are kept; examples and restatement usually go.

**4.** (a) practical weaknesses (b) communicable diseases

**5.** For example: does nothing connected with the craft.

**6.** For example: it cannot be proved to anyone elsewhere.

**7.** Marked on the count and on the central idea surviving.

**8.** Add nothing, omit nothing important, keep the proportions.

**9.** The claim, an exact quotation, and the word doing the work.

**10.** A writer who admitted a fault must be shown admitting it; dropping it changes the argument.

#### Challenge

**1.** The surviving sentence should be the passage's central idea.

**2.** A paraphrase changes the structure of the sentence, not a single word; changing one word usually only introduces an error.

**3.** Three claims, each with exact words and a location.

**4.** The limit tests selection, which is the skill being examined; anything important placed after it has not been selected.

**5.** Marked on the diagnosis rather than on the summary itself.

#### Exam-style

<!-- page 15 -->

**1.** The one thing the paragraph is chiefly saying; every other sentence must support it. **[3]**

**2.** Reasons, evidence, examples, restatement; reasons and evidence survive. **[4]**

**3.** (a) practical weaknesses (b) communicable diseases **[2]**

**4.** Add nothing, omit nothing important, keep the proportions. **[3]**

**5.** Marked on count, central idea and absence of opinion. **[5]**

**6.** Claim, exact quotation, and the word that carries the meaning. **[3]**

#### Writing task — marking guidance

Ten marks: within the limit with the count shown (2), central idea present (2), proportions kept and concessions retained (2), own words (2), three cited claims with exact located words (2). A summary over the limit is marked only as far as the limit.

#### Speaking and listening — what to look for

- **The spoken summary.** If listeners cannot state the central idea, the summary reported details instead.

- **The evidence court.** Learners discover how many of their confident claims have nothing in the text behind them. That discovery is the lesson.

- Sign the log only after the task has been performed aloud.

#### Marking notes and common errors

- **Word counts written down**, every time.

- **Concessions.** Dropping the writer's admissions is the commonest way a summary distorts.

- **Lifting.** Close the book before writing the sentence; it is the only reliable cure.

- **Core 7 and Exam 5** need a passage supplied by the teacher.

### Chapter 7: Word Classes and Their Functions

#### Recall

**1.** boxes

**2.** children

**3.** I know a tailor who works quickly.

**4.** a large old door

**5.** I will go / I shall go

**6.** until (or till)

**7.** carefully

**8.** married to

#### Key words

**1.** compound

**2.** head

**3.** relative

**4.** antecedent

**5.** defining

**6.** non-defining

**7.** pre-modify

**8.** Without commas the clause says which one is meant; with commas it merely adds a fact about something already identified. *My sister who lives in Tamale* implies I have more than one.

#### Core practice

**1.** sisters-in-law, passers-by, courts martial, runners-up, cupfuls

**2.** Three passers-by stopped. Both my mothers-in-law came.

**3.** (a) who (b) whose (c) whom

**4.** We thanked the driver whose bus was full.

**5.** With commas it means I have one uncle; without them it identifies which uncle is meant.

**6.** a beautiful white cotton dress; an ugly round metal tank

**7.** (a) future perfect (b) future progressive (c) simple future

**8.** (a) will be sitting (b) will have completed

**9.** (a) an adjective (b) an indefinite pronoun or determiner (c) a numeral

**10.** They discussed the fees. I am used to the noise. We reached Accra.

#### Challenge

**1.** The first replaced only the damaged ones; the second says all the books were damaged and all were replaced.

**2.** Only she lent it; she only lent it (did nothing more); she lent only him; she lent him only the money.

**3.** Simple, progressive, perfect and perfect progressive.

**4.** Five correct sentences, each using a suitable preposition.

**5.** English orders adjectives opinion, size, age, shape, colour…, so it should be *a beautiful big red cloth*.

#### Exam-style

**1.** passers-by, mothers-in-law, courts martial **[3]**

<!-- page 18 -->

**2.** Answer the clause with *he* or *him*; examples must match. **[3]**

**3.** With commas: one sister. Without: more than one, and the Tamale one is meant. **[4]**

**4.** a beautiful white cotton dress **[2]**

**5.** Simple, progressive, perfect, perfect progressive. **[4]**

**6.** discussed the fees; used to the noise; reached Accra **[4]**

#### Writing task — marking guidance

Ten marks: six compound nouns with three plurals (2), four relative clauses correctly punctuated (3), adjective order (2), all four futures (2), marginal marking accurate (1). The marking in the margin is the evidence of deliberate construction.

#### Speaking and listening — what to look for

- **The description game.** Adjective order is heard rather than reasoned; ask a learner who gets it wrong to say it again and listen.

- **The conversation.** The tick list is the assessment. A learner who cannot produce the perfect progressive naturally should be given a prompt.

- Sign the log only after the task has been performed aloud.

#### Marking notes and common errors

- **Head words.** Passers-by, not passer-bys. The rule is one sentence long and settles every compound plural.

- **Commas around relative clauses** change the meaning; they are never decorative.

- **Prepositions** must be learnt from sentences, not from lists. Table 7.12 of the Learner's Book is worth copying onto the wall.

- **Core 8.** Accept *shall* or *will* throughout; the older rule is expected in formal writing but both are heard.

*Strand 3: Grammar Usage*

### Chapter 8: Reported Speech and Question Tags

#### Recall

**1.** She said she was tired.

**2.** aren't you

**3.** is she

**4.** the next day

**5.** there

**6.** would

**7.** shall we

**8.** aren't I

#### Key words

**1.** direct

**2.** reported (or indirect)

**3.** backshift

**4.** reporting

**5.** tag

**6.** negative

**7.** intonation

**8.** The statement is still true when it is reported, so putting it into the past would suggest it had stopped being true.

#### Core practice

**1.** Ama said she was writing her examination the following week.

**2.** The men said they had finished the work.

**3.** The officer asked me where I lived.

**4.** She asked the boy to close the gate.

**5.** The electrician warned us not to touch the wires.

**6.** When the statement is still true, and when the reporting verb is in the present.

**7.** (a) hasn't she (b) did they (c) can't he

**8.** (a) shall we (b) will you (c) aren't I

**9.** (a) did they (b) was it (c) does he

**10.** Falling: the speaker expects agreement. Rising: the speaker is genuinely asking.

#### Challenge

**1.** Five reported sentences with five distinct verbs — said, asked, told, warned, promised, advised, admitted.

**2.** A reported question is no longer a question; the word order returns to subject then verb, and *did* disappears: *He asked me where I lived.*

**3.** *I am … aren't I*; *Let's … shall we*; imperative … *will you*; *Nobody … they*; *There is … isn't there*; *Everything … isn't it*.

**4.** Usually the speaker's exact tone and any striking phrasing.

**5.** The intonation intended must be marked.

#### Exam-style

**1.** Ama said she was writing her examination the following week. **[3]**

<!-- page 20 -->

**2.** The officer asked me where I lived. **[3]**

**3.** He warned us not to touch the wires. **[3]**

**4.** Take the auxiliary (or use do/does/did), reverse the polarity, replace the subject with a pronoun. **[3]**

**5.** (a) hasn't she (b) did they (c) shall we (d) aren't I **[4]**

**6.** Falling expects agreement; rising is a real question. **[4]**

#### Writing task — marking guidance

Ten marks: direct speech punctuated correctly (2), all four shifts made in the report (3), four reporting verbs (2), two correct tags (2), length (1). Marks for the shifts are lost one at a time — a report that changes tense but forgets the pronouns keeps two of the three.

#### Speaking and listening — what to look for

- **The messenger game.** Score the shifts, not the memory; allow the sentence to be repeated.

- **Tag intonation.** Learners hear the difference long before they can name it. Model both, then ask which one sounded like a real question.

- Sign the log only after the task has been performed aloud.

#### Marking notes and common errors
