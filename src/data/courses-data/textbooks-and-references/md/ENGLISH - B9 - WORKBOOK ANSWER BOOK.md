## How to use this answer book

Grammar, vocabulary and comprehension answers are given in full. Writing and speaking tasks cannot have single answers, so they carry marking guidance instead: what a strong response contains, and what to listen or look for.

Where more than one answer is acceptable the note says so. In language work this is the rule rather than the exception, and a learner who has produced something other than the answer printed here has not necessarily made a mistake.

The marking notes at the end of each chapter name the errors to expect. They are drawn from the misconceptions set out in the Learner's Book.

### Chapter 1: Speaking, Discussion and Debate

#### Recall

**1.** The level of formality a speaker chooses.

**2.** The statement a debate argues for and against.

**3.** The proposition.

**4.** An answer that shows why an opponent's argument fails.

**5.** Claim, reason and evidence.

**6.** Any question that cannot be answered yes or no.

**7.** Judges the debate and gives a decision.

**8.** Admitting that a point on the other side is correct.

#### Key words

**1.** linking

**2.** synthesis

**3.** proof

**4.** information

**5.** straw

**6.** ad hominem

**7.** floor

**8.** Both sides must be arguing about the same thing. If *free education* is left undefined, one side may argue about fees and the other about uniforms, and neither will meet the other.

#### Core practice

**1.** Three parts, and the link stated.

**2.** For example: *This matter must be resolved without delay, and I ask for your cooperation.*

**3.** Each must join a previous point to a new one.

**4.** Any versions beginning with what, why or how.

**5.** *Better* must be defined — by results, by cost, by welfare?

**6.** Name the hasty generalisation from a small sample.

**7.** One sentence, in question form, aimed at their evidence.

**8.** (a) ad hominem (b) straw man

**9.** The concession must be genuine, and the answer must still stand.

**10.** Keep order, give the floor fairly, keep to the point and to time, summarise.

#### Challenge

**1.** Marked on the evidence and the links.

**2.** Arguing against yourself is the exercise.

**3.** The proposition must show the motion is true; leaving it undecided is enough for the opposition.

**4.** Six statements, the difference visible in each pair.

**5.** Marked on the separation of the decision from the adjudicator's own opinion.

#### Exam-style

<!-- page 4 -->

**1.** Claim, reason, evidence; the link states how the evidence supports the claim. **[3]**

**2.** Three distinct parts. **[3]**

**3.** The statement debated; without definitions the two sides argue about different things. **[3]**

**4.** They said; but; because; therefore. **[4]**

**5.** (a) ad hominem (b) straw man **[4]**

**6.** The duty to prove the motion; it lies with the proposition. **[3]**

#### Writing task — marking guidance

Ten marks: terms defined (2), three arguments with all three parts (3), links stated (2), a four-step rebuttal (2), an honest concession (1). A speech that asserts three times without evidence earns the definition marks only.

#### Speaking and listening — what to look for

- **Points of information** are where debates are won and lost. Insist that every speaker takes at least one; a speaker who refuses them all should be marked down.

- **The discussion.** Synthesis is the hardest of the three requirements; model it once before the groups begin.

- Sign the log only after the task has been performed aloud.

#### Marking notes and common errors

- **Contradiction is not rebuttal.** This is the single most useful thing to teach in the chapter, and it transfers directly to the essay paper.

- **Straw men** are usually accidental at this age; treat them as a fault of listening rather than of honesty.

- **Register.** Learners over-correct into stiffness. Formal does not mean long.

- **Challenge 5** needs a debate to have been watched — the class debate in the speaking section will serve.

### Chapter 2: Listening to Audio-Visual Texts

#### Recall

**1.** A factual film about real people, places or events.

**2.** A voice heard while something else is shown on screen.

**3.** Words on screen naming or explaining what is shown.

**4.** A short recorded extract of someone speaking.

**5.** Figures and who said them.

**6.** A one-sided treatment of a subject.

**7.** The person being questioned.

**8.** For example: *How do you know that?*

#### Key words

**1.** audio-visual

**2.** footage

**3.** commentary

**4.** attribution

**5.** initiating

**6.** building

**7.** probing

**8.** A camera can only point one way. Choosing which people, places and moments appear tells the viewer what matters, before a word of commentary is spoken.

#### Core practice

**1.** Footage, commentary, voice-over, captions, sound bites, interviews, music, graphics — any six.

**2.** Marked on whether the fourth column has anything in it.

**3.** Three figures, three attributions.

**4.** Two genuine differences between image and word.

**5.** Music, choice of images, emotive words, an expert's authority, repetition, ordering.

**6.** Two genuinely absent groups.

**7.** It must press on evidence or on selection.

**8.** Three statements correctly marked.

**9.** Central idea, main evidence, count given.

**10.** Figures, sources and questions; what remains is an impression.

#### Challenge

**1.** Differences of selection, order, emphasis or language.

**2.** Through selection, order, emphasis and omission, none of which requires a false statement.

**3.** The script must show what is on screen as well as what is said.

**4.** The exercise proves the point of Challenge 2.

**5.** Marked on the record.

#### Exam-style

**1.** Any six with contributions. **[4]**

<!-- page 6 -->

**2.** Facts and figures; who said it; what the pictures showed; my questions. **[4]**

**3.** Naming the source; without it the figure cannot be checked or weighed. **[3]**

**4.** Music, image selection, emotive language, expert authority, repetition, ordering. **[3]**

**5.** Through selection, emphasis and omission. **[3]**

**6.** Starting a line; adding to another's point; pressing for evidence or explanation. **[3]**

#### Writing task — marking guidance

Ten marks: four facts with sources (3), image separated from word (2), two techniques with examples (2), an absent voice named (2), a genuine unanswered question (1). A review that reports only what was said, with no notice of what was shown, loses a third of the marks.

#### Speaking and listening — what to look for

- **The ninety-second report.** If the group cannot state the central claim, the report gave impressions rather than content.

- **The discussion tally.** Building on is the requirement learners find hardest; most groups produce parallel speeches rather than a discussion.

- Sign the log only after the task has been performed aloud.

#### Marking notes and common errors

- **Radio works as well as television** for every task here except the image column; where screens are unavailable, substitute a recorded interview and drop that column.

- **Selection, not falsehood.** This is the central idea of the chapter and it will be tested again in Chapter 4.

- **Four columns.** Learners resist the fourth column until they try to write the review without it.

- **Core 2 to 9** all depend on one broadcast; choose it carefully and use it for the whole chapter.

### Chapter 3: Sounds, Clusters and Stress

#### Recall

**1.** Three.

**2.** On the first syllable.

**3.** /str/

**4.** /skt/

**5.** Voiced.

**6.** light / right — or any correct pair.

**7.** A word of one syllable.

**8.** The second.

#### Key words

**1.** lateral

**2.** approximant

**3.** cluster

**4.** insertion

**5.** reduction

**6.** syllable

**7.** shift

**8.** The stressed word is the one being contrasted with something else. *I* didn't take it points at someone else; I didn't take *it* points at something else.

#### Core practice

**1.** For example: light, follow, tall.

**2.** red, arrive, sorry; in non-rhotic accents final *r* is not pronounced before a pause.

**3.** right, correct, grass, yet

**4.** /str/, /spl/, /skr/, /lfθs/, /rldz/

**5.** The first two insert a vowel into the cluster; the third reduces and reorders it.

**6.** 3, 3, 5, 3, 5

**7.** As shown.

**8.** First syllable for the nouns, second for the verbs.

**9.** (a) someone else did (b) something else was taken (c) it was not taken but perhaps moved or borrowed

**10.** Marked by ear.

#### Challenge

**1.** The reduced consonant carries tense or number, so the grammar of the sentence disappears with the sound.

**2.** Twelve pairs, correctly stressed when read aloud.

**3.** Five meanings from one sentence.

**4.** The suffix decides the stress; e.g. *economy, economic, economist*; *politics, political, politician*.

**5.** Marked on the honesty of the list.

#### Exam-style

**1.** /str/, /lfθs/, /skt/ **[3]**

<!-- page 8 -->

**2.** Adding a vowel inside a cluster; dropping a consonant from it. **[4]**

**3.** 3, 4, 5; imPORtant, phoTOgrapher, opporTUnity **[3]**

**4.** First syllable for nouns, second for verbs. **[3]**

**5.** Someone else did; something else was taken. **[4]**

**6.** The /t/ carries the past tense. **[3]**

#### Writing task — marking guidance

Ten marks: ten clusters used and underlined (3), four final clusters carrying grammar (3), four stress-shift words used in both classes (3), stress marked (1). Mark the underlining by sound rather than by spelling: *box* ends in a cluster although it is written with one letter.

#### Speaking and listening — what to look for

- **Mark the three things only.** Do not deduct for a Ghanaian accent; deduct where a cluster has lost a consonant that carries meaning.

- **The dictation.** Back to back, so lips cannot be read.

- Sign the log only after the task has been performed aloud.

#### Marking notes and common errors

- **Clusters carry grammar.** By Basic 9 this should be automatic; it is worth two or three minutes at the start of every lesson.

- **Stress shift** appears in the BECE and is easily taught; twelve pairs learnt properly cover most of what is asked.

- **Sentence stress** is the most enjoyable part of the chapter and the least practised. Core 9 should be done aloud before it is written.

- **Challenge 5** needs a recording device; a shared telephone will do.

### Chapter 4: Reading Widely and Independently

#### Recall

**1.** To tell readers what happened.

**2.** An article giving the newspaper's own opinion.

**3.** A statement issued by an organisation to be reported.

**4.** The position from which a writer sees a subject.

**5.** Who wrote it, when, how they know, who else says so — any one.

**6.** Confirmation from a second, independent source.

**7.** A statement made about a whole group.

**8.** Something a source stands to gain.

#### Key words

**1.** feature

**2.** column

**3.** hasty

**4.** false

**5.** dilemma

**6.** popularity

**7.** circular

**8.** A press release is written by the organisation it is about, to be believed. A news report should have been checked by someone with nothing to gain from it.

#### Core practice

**1.** News report, feature, editorial, opinion column, press release.

**2.** (a) news report or press release (b) feature (c) editorial

**3.** Three of each, correctly sorted.

**4.** Hasty generalisation, false cause, false dilemma, appeal to popularity, circular reasoning.

**5.** (a) hasty generalisation (b) false cause (c) false dilemma

**6.** (a) appeal to popularity (b) circular reasoning

**7.** Four answers, drawn from the text.

**8.** A second, independent source must be named.

**9.** Something the source stands to gain; two plausible examples.

**10.** Marked on completeness.

#### Challenge

**1.** Differences of fact, source, emphasis, order or language.

**2.** Marked on the identification.

**3.** The fallacies must be named.

**4.** An unnamed many cannot be checked or weighed; a named source can be examined for interest and for knowledge.

**5.** An editorial with no judgements is a report; with no facts it is an assertion.

#### Exam-style

**1.** News report, feature, editorial, opinion column, press release. **[4]**

<!-- page 11 -->

**2.** Issued by the body it concerns, to be believed; it serves that body's interest. **[3]**

**3.** Hasty generalisation, false cause, false dilemma, appeal to popularity, circular reasoning. **[3]**

**4.** hasty generalisation; false cause; false dilemma **[3]**

**5.** Who wrote it and what do they gain; when; how do they know; who else says so. **[4]**

**6.** Confirmation by an independent source; six repetitions of one source remain one source. **[3]**

#### Writing task — marking guidance

Ten marks: both sources correctly classified (2), facts separated from judgements (2), four credibility questions applied to both (3), a fallacy correctly named (2), a reasoned choice (1). A candidate who prefers the source they agree with, without reasons drawn from the four questions, loses the last three marks.

#### Speaking and listening — what to look for

- **The challenging question** should press on why the chosen source is more credible, not on the subject itself.

- **The fallacy hunt** works best with real arguments cut from newspapers or heard on radio.

- Sign the log only after the task has been performed aloud.

#### Marking notes and common errors

- **Five fallacies, learnt by name.** They recur in the BECE comprehension and in every debate; naming them is half the work of answering them.

- **Press releases** reported verbatim are common; Challenge 2 is easy to set from any week's papers.

- **Credibility is not agreement.** Learners must practise finding a source credible while disagreeing with it.

- **Core 10 and Challenge 1** run over two weeks; set them at the start of the term.

### Chapter 5: Comprehending and Analysing Texts

#### Recall

**1.** Searching a text for one particular piece of information.

**2.** Reading quickly for the general sense.

**3.** Cause and effect, problem and solution, compare and contrast, chronological, general to particular — any one.

**4.** A comparison using *like* or *as*.

**5.** Language appealing to the five senses.

**6.** The associations a word carries beyond its plain meaning.

**7.** Something that stands for an idea beyond itself.

**8.** Because, therefore, consequently, as a result — any one.

#### Key words

**1.** structure

**2.** problem

**3.** general

**4.** style

**5.** purpose

**6.** domain-specific

**7.** nuance

**8.** Naming a device and describing a structure can both be done without understanding the text. Effect asks what the choice does to the reader, which cannot be answered from a list.

#### Core practice

**1.** (a) scanning (b) skimming (c) close reading

**2.** Cause and effect (because); problem and solution (however, the answer is); compare and contrast (whereas); chronological (then, next); general to particular (for instance).

**3.** Three structures with evidence.

**4.** A problem stated in paragraph one predicts a solution; a comparison predicts the second term.

**5.** Two three-move answers.

**6.** Two explanations, each with the words quoted.

**7.** The symbol and the idea must both be named.

**8.** Dying or dead; the clue is the explanation that follows.

**9.** Weather; land or employment; water or schools; money sent home by migrants.

**10.** Each pair shares a meaning and differs in approval.

#### Challenge

**1.** Function, not content: *concedes*, *gives evidence*, *narrows*.

**2.** Weak names the device; fair describes it; strong says what it does to the reader, with words quoted.

**3.** The loss must be named precisely.

**4.** Twelve correct uses.

**5.** Four genuine shifts of connotation.

#### Exam-style

<!-- page 13 -->

**1.** Cause and effect; problem and solution; compare and contrast; chronological; general to particular. **[4]**

**2.** Scanning; skimming; close reading. **[3]**

**3.** What the writer does; how it works; what it does to the reader. **[3]**

**4.** A three-move answer, with the effect named. **[4]**

**5.** Dying or dead; the explanation that follows. **[3]**

**6.** Same denotation, opposite approval in each pair. **[3]**

#### Writing task — marking guidance

Ten marks: purpose and audience (1), structure traced with quoted signals (3), three language choices in all three moves (3), a symbol identified (1), four connotations explained (2). An analysis that names devices without saying what they do earns half the language marks at most.

#### Speaking and listening — what to look for

- **The three-minute analysis.** Require the words to be quoted aloud; a learner who cannot find them has claimed an effect that is not there.

- **Naming paragraph functions** in one word is a fast drill and prepares Chapter 6 directly.

- Sign the log only after the task has been performed aloud.

#### Marking notes and common errors

- **Effect is the BECE discriminator.** Most candidates name; few explain. Three moves, every time.

- **Structure predicts.** A learner who recognises problem and solution in paragraph one has already half-read the article.

- **Domain-specific vocabulary** is where reading across the curriculum pays; take examples from Science and Social Studies.

- **Core 3 to 9** work best on one shared article.

### Chapter 6: Analysing a Whole Text and Summarising

#### Recall

**1.** The one thing the paragraph is chiefly saying.

**2.** The claim a whole text sets out to establish.

**3.** Cereals.

**4.** Containing none of the summariser's opinion.

**5.** A restatement in your own words at similar length.

**6.** Its source or location.

**7.** Six.

**8.** Something taken for granted rather than argued.

#### Key words

**1.** whole-text

**2.** thesis

**3.** function

**4.** assumption

**5.** proportions

**6.** distortion

**7.** omission

**8.** A summary that drops the writer's concession makes them sound certain when they were careful. Nothing false has been written, and the reader has still been misled.

#### Core practice

**1.** One sentence covering the whole text.

**2.** Six functions, not six summaries.

**3.** Usually two that perform the same function.

**4.** It must be unstated in the text.

**5.** (a) infrastructure (b) health workers (c) natural hazards

**6.** For example: *It failed because nobody kept accounts, not because funds ran out.*

**7.** Marked on count, thesis, proportions and concessions.

**8.** The final sentence should be the thesis.

**9.** Add nothing (opinion creeps in); omit nothing important (concessions go); keep the proportions (one vivid example takes half the space).

**10.** Two claims, each with evidence.

#### Challenge

**1.** The summary should follow the map's proportions.

**2.** Name the omission or the added emphasis.

**3.** Three unstated premises, ranked.

**4.** The content should not change; the emphasis and the register may.

**5.** Marked on the diagnosis.

#### Exam-style

<!-- page 15 -->

**1.** The claim of a whole text; a central idea belongs to one paragraph. **[3]**

**2.** What it does for the argument; claims, concedes, illustrates, narrows. **[3]**

**3.** Add nothing; omit nothing important; keep the proportions. **[4]**

**4.** (a) infrastructure (b) natural hazards **[2]**

**5.** Marked on count, thesis, proportions and absence of opinion. **[5]**

**6.** That nothing else changed at the same time. **[3]**

#### Writing task — marking guidance

Ten marks: summary within the limit with the count (2), thesis stated (2), proportions and concessions kept (2), paragraph functions named rather than summarised (2), two located quotations (2). Marking stops at the word limit.

#### Speaking and listening — what to look for

- **The spoken summary.** If listeners report details but not the thesis, the summary was a list.

- **The assumption hunt** teaches learners to separate *what must be true for this to follow* from *what I think about it*. That separation is the whole chapter.

- Sign the log only after the task has been performed aloud.

#### Marking notes and common errors

- **Function, not content.** Learners write a second summary in the function column unless the one-word rule is enforced.

- **Concessions** are the first casualty of a short summary and the commonest distortion.

- **Word counts written down**, every time, in every task.

- **Core 7 and Exam 5** need a passage supplied by the teacher; a BECE past paper passage is ideal.

### Chapter 7: Phrases and Their Functions

#### Recall

**1.** A group of words working as a unit, with no subject and verb.

**2.** boy

**3.** Uncountable.

**4.** much rice

**5.** put off

**6.** a large red box

**7.** very quietly

**8.** Moving a phrase to the front of the sentence.

#### Key words

**1.** phrase

**2.** head

**3.** pre-modifier

**4.** post-modifier

**5.** quantifier

**6.** particle

**7.** separable

**8.** A clause has a subject and a verb of its own and can often stand alone. A phrase is a unit without them, and works inside a clause as a single part of speech.

#### Core practice

**1.** (a) phrase (b) clause (c) phrase (d) clause

**2.** man; bag; three

**3.** For example: *the elderly market woman who sells yam*.

**4.** a beautiful white cotton dress; an old round metal tank

**5.** (a) much (b) many (c) much or little (d) many or few

**6.** much advice; fewer pupils; a little money

**7.** postpone; visit or invite to speak; stop trying; meet by chance; care for; raise a child or mention

**8.** turn it off; look after the child; put it off

**9.** manner; place; frequency; time

**10.** For example: *Slowly and without lights, the lorry arrived at dawn.* The emphasis moves to the manner.

#### Challenge

**1.** Determiner, pre-modifiers, head, post-modifier.

**2.** *Fewer* for countables, *less* for uncountables.

**3.** Ten verbs, correctly sorted, each tested with a pronoun.

**4.** Four versions, four emphases.

**5.** The whole subject is a single noun phrase built on *boy*, with one pre-modifier and two post-modifiers.

#### Exam-style

**1.** A clause has a subject and verb of its own. **[3]**

<!-- page 18 -->

**2.** man; bag **[2]**

**3.** a beautiful white cotton dress **[2]**

**4.** much advice; fewer pupils; a little money **[4]**

**5.** postpone; visit or invite; stop trying; care for. *Look after* and *call on* are inseparable. **[5]**

**6.** A correct fronting and the shift of emphasis. **[4]**

#### Writing task — marking guidance

Ten marks: four long noun phrases underlined (3), six phrasal verbs with correct pronoun placement (3), four kinds of adverbial (2), two fronted adverbials (1), adjective order (1). *Turn off it* costs the phrasal-verb marks however many verbs are used.

#### Speaking and listening — what to look for

- **The noun phrase game.** Learners discover that post-modifiers identify faster than adjectives do.

- **Forced phrasal verbs** are easy to hear. Six natural ones beat ten crammed in.

- Sign the log only after the task has been performed aloud.

#### Marking notes and common errors

- **Fewer and less** is examined most years and is worth teaching as one line: countable, *fewer*; uncountable, *less*.

- **Phrasal verbs** cannot be derived; they must be met in sentences and recorded. Keep a class list on the wall.

- **Pronoun placement** is the practical test of separability, and the fastest way to mark it.

- **Fronting** is a style tool, not a rule. Two fronted adverbials in a paragraph is a mannerism.

### Chapter 8: Conditionals, Sentences and Voice

#### Recall

**1.** will stay

**2.** would accept

**3.** The window was broken by the boy.

**4.** The tall man in the blue overall.

**5.** Each of the pupils was present.

**6.** A comma follows the if-clause.

**7.** The doer, introduced by *by*.

**8.** The contractor repaired the road.

#### Key words

**1.** conditional

**2.** if-clause

**3.** second

**4.** third

**5.** mixed

**6.** concord

**7.** agentless

**8.** The agentless passive states the fact and hides the doer. Nothing false is said and nobody is named, which is exactly why officials reach for it.

#### Core practice

**1.** (a) will be (b) had (c) would have come

**2.** (a) zero conditional — a general truth (b) second — unreal, I am not the head teacher

**3.** A past condition with a present result.

**4.** If the road is closed, we shall walk. We shall walk if the road is closed.

**5.** Without commas, only those who had paid were admitted; with commas, all had paid and all were admitted.

**6.** Subject: The three lorries carrying cocoa (simple subject *lorries*). Predicate: left before dawn.

**7.** Each … has; Neither … is; The committee have taken their seats (or *has taken its seats*).

**8.** (a) The market was closed by the Assembly. (b) My bag has been stolen. (c) The results will be announced on Friday.

**9.** (a) The secretary wrote the letter. (b) Someone has reduced the fees — the agent must be supplied.

**10.** When the doer is unknown, unimportant, or obvious; in scientific method; when the receiver matters more.

#### Challenge

**1.** First real; second unreal now; third unreal past; mixed.

**2.** *Were* is the subjunctive, kept for unreal conditions. Speech has largely lost it; formal writing and examinations have not.

**3.** Usually clarity and the identity of the doer.

**4.** Three genuine examples with the missing agent named.

**5.** Six correct sentences.

#### Exam-style

<!-- page 20 -->

**1.** will be; had; would have come **[3]**

**2.** First real; second unreal present; third unreal past; mixed across two times. **[4]**

**3.** Without commas only the payers were admitted; with commas all had paid. **[3]**

**4.** has; is **[3]**

**5.** The market was closed by the Assembly. My bag has been stolen. **[4]**

**6.** Doer unknown, unimportant or obvious; the agentless passive in official statements. **[3]**

#### Writing task — marking guidance

Ten marks: three conditionals correctly formed (3), if-clause punctuation (1), two relative clauses correctly punctuated (2), three passives with the agentless one marked (2), the explanation of the choice (2). The last two marks are the point of the task: the passive is a decision, not an accident.

#### Speaking and listening — what to look for

- **The regret game** produces the third and mixed conditionals faster than any exercise on paper.

- **Active and passive aloud.** Learners hear the evasion at once, which is the lesson.

- Sign the log only after the task has been performed aloud.

#### Marking notes and common errors

- **If I were you.** Teach it as the form expected in writing, without calling spoken *was* an error.

- **Concord traps** — *each*, *neither*, *the number of* — are examined most years and are worth a wall chart.

- **The passive is a choice.** Basic 9 should be able to say why it was chosen, not merely form it.

- **Challenge 4** is best set from a real government or company statement.
