## Preface

English is the one subject you cannot put down at the end of the lesson. You carry it into every other subject, into every office you will ever walk into, and into every conversation you have outside your own home. That is why this book spends as much time on speaking and listening as on writing, and why so many of its tasks ask you to perform something rather than only to hand it in.

The book is written so that the connection to the national curriculum is visible on every page rather than hidden in a scheme of work. Each chapter opens with the strand, sub-strand, content standard, indicators and core competences it teaches, quoted word for word from the NaCCA curriculum. Nothing is paraphrased, and nothing is claimed that the curriculum does not say.

Every chapter is built on the same spine: an explanation, then models set out so that you can see exactly what was changed and why, then a Practice exercise on that same skill. Where the curriculum gives an exemplar — identify a formal situation, give directions to a familiar place, match a Ghanaian proverb to an English one, compose an advertisement — that exemplar has been turned into something you can actually carry out.

The assessment at the end of each chapter follows the modes the curriculum itself sets out. The English curriculum states no percentage weightings; it gives five levels of proficiency and a list of assessment modes. Accordingly each chapter ends with four sections: an objective test, a written response, a listening and speaking task, and a piece for your portfolio. Two of those four cannot be done sitting down, which is the point.

## How to use this book

Every chapter follows the same order, so that once you are used to it you can find anything quickly.

**Practice —** questions on exactly the skill just shown.

**Activities —** practical work, with what you need, what to do and what to record.

**Apply it —** a longer task that uses the whole chapter.

**Chapter summary —** the whole chapter in ten lines.

**Assessment —** Sections A, B, C and D.

#### The four assessment sections

Section A is an objective test. Section B is theory and written response. Section C is a listening and speaking task, carried out aloud and assessed as you perform it. Section D is a portfolio or project piece, collected over a longer period. All four are named in the curriculum’s own list of assessment modes.

#### Speaking and listening

Section C cannot be answered in a book. It has to be done aloud, in front of somebody. If you skip those sections you will have studied English rather than learnt it, and the difference will show the first time you have to explain yourself to a stranger.

#### Learning objectives

By the end of this chapter you can:

- choose a register that suits both the occasion and the person you are addressing

- sustain a formal conversation with an official, a stranger or a panel

- ask questions that link what two or more speakers have said

- summarise a discussion fairly before adding your own view

- use open-ended questions to open up national and global issues

- distinguish a claim, a reason and evidence in what a speaker says

- state and defend a position in a formal debate

- rebut an argument by attacking the reason rather than the person

- offer, accept and decline a point of information courteously

- chair or time a debate and judge it against stated criteria

#### Key words

> **Register —** the level of formality a speaker chooses.
>
> **Linking question —** a question that joins what two or more speakers have said.
>
> **Synthesis —** a fair statement of what a discussion has established so far.
>
> **Open-ended question —** a question that cannot be answered with yes, no or one word.
>
> **Claim —** a statement a speaker asks you to accept.
>
> **Reason —** the ground on which a claim rests.
>
> **Evidence —** the facts offered in support of a reason.
>
> **Motion —** the statement a debate is held on, beginning \*This house…\*
>
> **Proposition —** the side arguing for the motion.
>
> **Opposition —** the side arguing against the motion.
>
> **Rebuttal —** an answer that shows why an opponent's argument fails.
>
> **Point of information —** a brief question offered to a speaker while they hold the floor.

> **Burden of proof —** the duty of the side that must prove the case.
>
> **Definition —** the meaning the proposition assigns to the terms of the motion.
>
> **Straw man —** attacking a weaker argument than the one actually made.
>
> **Ad hominem —** attacking the speaker instead of the argument.
>

> **Floor —** the right to speak, held by one speaker at a time.

#### Engage

In two years' time somebody will disagree with you about something that matters — a mark, a wage, a decision that affects your family — and you will have about ninety seconds to make your case to a person who does not have to listen. Everything in this chapter is practice for those ninety seconds. Debating looks like a competition with bells and formal language. What it teaches is much plainer: how to know what you actually think, and how to say it in a form somebody else can weigh.

#### 1.1 Register with diverse partners

By now the principle is familiar: the occasion sets the least formality permitted, and the person you are addressing decides how far above that you stand. At Basic 9 the range of partners widens — a panel interviewing you, a radio presenter, an official who has no reason to help you, an audience that disagrees with you.

| **Partner** | **Situation** | **What the register must do** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| An interview panel | a placement or a scholarship | confident without boasting; complete sentences |
| A radio presenter | a live contribution | brief, clear, no jargon, no rambling |
| An official who is unwilling | a request that has been refused once | courteous, factual, persistent |
| An audience that disagrees | a debate or a meeting | respectful of them, firm on the point |
| A younger learner | explaining something | plain, patient, without condescension |
| A peer in a group task | planning work | cooperative; questions rather than instructions |

*Table 1.1 — Six partners and what each requires.*

One difference matters more at this level than any other. Speaking to somebody who **disagrees** with you is not the same as speaking to somebody who does not yet know. The first needs concessions and evidence; the second needs explanation. Learners who confuse the two either lecture people who are already convinced, or explain patiently to people who are waiting to argue.

#### 1.2 Questions that link speakers

In Basic 8 you asked open-ended questions. The Basic 9 skill is harder: asking a question that **links** what two or more people have said. A linking question shows you have been listening to everybody, and it moves a discussion forward instead of restarting it.

| **Kind of linking question** | **Form** | **Example** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Joining two speakers | *X said… and Y said… — are those the* *same point?* | *Ama says the fees are too high and* *Kojo says the money is misspent. Are* *those the same complaint?* |
| Testing a disagreement | *Where exactly do you two differ?* | *You both want the dump moved. Do* *you differ on where it should go, or on* |

<!-- page 10 -->

|  |  | *who should pay?* |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bringing in the silent | *Yaa, you have heard both. Which is* *nearer your experience?* | — |
| Pushing for evidence | *You have each given a reason. Which* *of you has a figure?* | — |
| Naming the assumption | *You are both assuming the assembly* *will act. Should we?* | — |

*Table 1.2 — Five questions that link.*

Before a linking question comes a **synthesis** — a short, fair statement of what has been said. Fairness is the whole of it: if your summary favours one side, the discussion stops being a discussion. *So far we have two positions. Kojo says the money exists but is misdirected; Ama says there is not enough of it. Both agree the school has not been paid.*

> **Dialogue 1.1 — A class discussion on climate change**
>
> **Teacher:** We have twenty minutes on the motion that Ghana should stop issuing licences for small-scale mining. Kojo.
>
> **Kojo:** The rivers are the point. My grandmother could drink from the Offin. Nobody drinks from it now.
>
> **Efua:** And nobody eats without money. Twelve people in my area depend on one pit. Close it and you have not saved the river; you have added twelve hungry families to the same district.
>
> **Kojo:** So we poison the water to feed people who will need the water?
>
> **Teacher:** Yaa, you have heard both. Where exactly do they differ?
>
> **Yaa:** They do not differ about the river. Neither of them says the river is fine. They differ about what should happen to the twelve families, and neither has said.
>
> **Efua:** That is fair. I have not said, because I do not know.
>
> **Kojo:** Nor do I.
>
> **Yaa:** Then can I ask both of you something? If a scheme paid those families for two years to do something else, would you both accept the ban?
>
> **Efua:** I would.
>
> **Kojo:** So would I, and I would want to know who pays for it.
>
> **Teacher:** In one sentence, then, what has this class established?
>
> **Yaa:** That we agree on the problem, we agree on a condition for solving it, and we do not know how it is funded.

Yaa speaks four times and never states an opinion of her own. She locates the disagreement, names what nobody has said, puts a question that both sides can answer, and sums up. That is the most valuable thing anyone does in that dialogue.

> **Practice 1.1**
>
> 1. From Dialogue 1.1, write the synthesis Yaa gives, and explain why it is fair to both speakers.
>
> 2. Write one linking question of each of the five kinds in Table 1.2, for a discussion on social media.
>
> 3. Two speakers say: *(a) Sanitation is a matter for the assembly. (b) People throw rubbish because there are no bins.* Are these the same point? Write the question you would ask.
>
> 4. Explain the difference between speaking to somebody who disagrees with you and somebody who does not yet know, and give one thing you would do differently in each.

#### 1.3 Discussing national and global issues

A discussion of a large issue collapses in one of two ways: everybody agrees and nothing is learnt, or everybody asserts and nobody listens. Three habits prevent both.

**Separate claim, reason and evidence.** *Social media harms young people* is a claim. *Because it replaces*

- *sleep* is a reason. *A survey of our two Form 3 classes found an average of five hours a night* is evidence. Most arguments you will hear consist of claims only. **Narrow the question until it can be answered.** *Is climate change real?* goes nowhere in a classroom.

- *Should our district ban farming within thirty metres of a river?* can be argued with what people actually know. **Say what would change your mind.** A speaker who cannot answer that question is not discussing; they

- are announcing.

| **Weak question** | **Better question** |
| --- | --- |
| Is corruption bad? | Which is the more effective check on corruption — prosecution or publication? |
| Should we protect the environment? | Should sand winning be licensed, banned, or taxed? |
| Is the media useful? | In what way does a radio station serve a district that a newspaper cannot? |
| Is the UN important? | What can the UN do about a border dispute that ECOWAS cannot? |
| Are drugs bad? | Should a first offence of drug use be treated as a health matter or a criminal one? |

*Table 1.3 — Narrowing an issue until it can be discussed.*

#### 1.4 Debate

A **debate** is a discussion under rules. The rules exist so that both sides are heard for the same length of time, and so that the argument is judged rather than shouted down.

##### 1.4.1 The parts of a debate

| **Role** | **What they do** |
| --- | --- |
| The motion | the statement being argued, beginning *This house believes* *that…* |
| First proposition | defines the terms, states the case for, gives two or three arguments |
| First opposition | accepts or challenges the definition, rebuts, gives the case against |
| Second speakers | rebut the other side, add one new argument, defend their own case |
| Reply speeches | no new argument; a summary of why your side won |
| Chairperson | keeps order, allocates the floor, calls time |
| Timekeeper | signals at one minute remaining and at time |
| Adjudicator | judges on argument, evidence, rebuttal and delivery — not on which side they agree with |

*Table 1.4 — The roles in a formal debate.*

##### 1.4.2 Building an argument

Every argument in a debate should be built the same way, and it is worth learning the shape by heart, because it works in essays, letters and interviews too.

<!-- page 12 -->

**State it.** *Our first argument is that a ban would protect the water supply of four districts.*

- **Explain it.** *When mercury enters a river it does not settle; it travels downstream, so the damage is not*

- *confined to the district that licensed the pit.* **Evidence it.** A figure, an example, a case, an authority.

- **Link it back.** *This is why the motion should stand: the harm crosses the boundary of the people who*

- *benefit.*

##### 1.4.3 Rebuttal

**Rebuttal** is answering the other side. It is what separates a debate from two speeches. A rebuttal names the argument, then shows what is wrong with it.

| **Kind of rebuttal** | **What it says** |
| --- | --- |
| It is not true | the evidence is wrong, out of date, or does not say what they claim |
| It does not follow | the evidence is true but the conclusion is not supported by it |
| It is not enough | true, but small beside the harm on our side |
| It applies to us both | their objection is equally a problem for their own case |
| We accept it, and | concede the point, and show the motion still stands |

*Table 1.5 — Five kinds of rebuttal.*

> **Model 1.1 — A weak rebuttal and a strong one**
>
> **Their argument:** *A ban will put twelve families in my area out of work.*
>
> **Weak:** *That is not true. The opposition is only thinking about money and not about the environment. They do not care about the future of this country.*
>
> **Strong:** *We accept the twelve families entirely — it is the strongest thing the opposition has said, and any honest proposition must answer it. Two things follow. First, the loss is temporary and the poisoning is not:*
>
> *mercury in a riverbed outlasts a pit by decades. Second, the opposition's own figure makes our case. Twelve families depend on that pit; four thousand people in three towns drink from the river below it. If we are counting families, we should count all of them.*
>
> **What changed:** the weak version denies without evidence and then attacks the speakers — an **ad hominem**, and the surest way to lose an adjudicator. The strong version concedes the point, which costs nothing and buys credibility, and then turns the opponent's own figure against them.

> **Common misconception 1.1**
>
> *Conceding a point is losing ground.* It is the opposite. An adjudicator watching a speaker deny something obviously true stops believing everything else that speaker says. Concede what is true, then show why it does not settle the matter — that is exactly what *We accept the twelve families entirely* achieves.

##### 1.4.4 Points of information

A **point of information** is a brief question offered to the speaker who holds the floor. You stand and say *On a point of information* — and the speaker may accept it or wave it down. Two rules: keep it to one sentence, and make it a question, not a speech.

| **Do** | **Do not** |
| --- | --- |
| Offer two or three across a debate | offer one every twenty seconds |
| Ask one clear question | make a second speech standing up |
| Accept one or two when you speak | accept every one and lose your own time |
| Decline politely — *No, thank you* | ignore the person entirely |

*Table 1.6 — Points of information.*

##### 1.4.5 Two faults that cost marks

**Straw man.** Answering an argument the other side did not make. *The opposition says the environment*

- *does not matter* — when what they said was that twelve families need feeding. **Ad hominem.** Attacking the speaker: their school, their accent, their sincerity. It never persuades an

- adjudicator and it usually persuades the audience against you.

> **Exam tip 1.1**
>
> In a written debate question, examiners look for the four-part argument — statement, explanation, evidence, link — and for at least one rebuttal that names the opposing argument before answering it. Write *The opposition will say that…* and you have earned the mark for rebuttal before you have answered anything.

> **Practice 1.2**
>
> 1. Build a four-part argument, in full sentences, for or against: *This house believes that mobile telephones should be allowed in JHS.*
>
> 2. Write a rebuttal of each of these, naming which of the five kinds you are using: (a) *Banning sachet water would create jobs in bottling.* (b) *Young people who use social media read less.* (c) *A curfew would stop drug abuse.*
>
> 3. Rewrite as a proper rebuttal: *The opposition does not care about our future and their figures are made up.*
>
> 4. Write three points of information you could offer against the argument in Model 1.1, each of one sentence.
>
> 5. Identify the fault: *The proposition says we should all become farmers.* (What was actually said: *farming should be made attractive to school leavers.*)

> **Activity 1.1 — The fishbowl discussion**
>
> You need: eight chairs in an inner circle, the rest of the class outside it; one issue from this year's themes.
>
> What to do:
>
> 1. Eight learners discuss the issue for ten minutes. Only they may speak.
>
> 2. Each learner outside the circle watches one speaker and records: claims made, reasons given, evidence offered, linking questions asked, and interruptions.
>
> 3. Halfway through, the teacher stops the discussion and one outside learner delivers a synthesis of what has been established. The circle says whether it was fair.
>
> 4. Swap the circles and repeat with a second issue.
>
> Record: the observation sheets and both syntheses.

<!-- page 14 -->

> Think about it: how many contributions were claims with no reason attached? In most classes it is more than half.

> **Activity 1.2 — A full class debate**
>
> You need: a motion; two teams of three; a chairperson, a timekeeper and three adjudicators.
>
> What to do:
>
> 1. Draw the motion and the side a week in advance. Nobody chooses their own side.
>
> 2. Each team prepares three arguments in the four-part shape, and predicts the other side's three.
>
> 3. Debate: four minutes for each substantive speech, two for each reply. Points of information allowed except in the first and last minute.
>
> 4. Adjudicators score argument, evidence, rebuttal and delivery out of five each, and must justify the result in two minutes.
>
> Record: the preparation notes, the score sheets and the adjudication.
>
> Think about it: arguing a side you disagree with is uncomfortable. What did you discover about your own position by having to attack it?

> **Apply it — The issue file**
>
> Choose one national or global issue from this year's themes.
>
> (a) Narrow it to a question that can actually be argued, as in Table 1.3.
>
> (b) Write three arguments for and three against, each in the four-part shape.
>
> (c) For each argument, write the rebuttal the other side would offer, and your answer to it.
>
> (d) Gather one piece of real evidence — a figure, a report, an interview you conduct yourself.
>
> (e) Write one paragraph stating what would change your mind.
>
> (f) Argue the side you disagree with in a class debate, then say afterwards which of your own arguments now looks weakest.

#### Chapter summary

- The occasion sets the least formality allowed; the partner decides how far above it you stand.

- Speaking to somebody who disagrees needs concessions and evidence; speaking to somebody who does not yet know needs explanation.

- A linking question joins what two or more speakers have said and moves a discussion forward.

- A fair synthesis must be acceptable to both sides, or the discussion stops being one.

- Separate claim, reason and evidence: most of what you hear consists of claims only.

- Narrow a large issue until it can be answered with what people actually know.

- Every argument has four parts: state it, explain it, evidence it, link it back.

- Rebuttal names the opposing argument and then shows it is untrue, does not follow, is not enough, applies to both sides, or is true but not decisive.

- Conceding what is true buys credibility; denying it destroys credibility for everything else you say.

- Straw man and ad hominem lose debates: answer the argument that was made, and never the person who made it.

#### Engage

A three-minute television report on a flood shows water in a street, a woman standing on a table, and a man in a suit promising drains. You will remember the woman on the table for years. You will not remember the figure the man gave, and there was one. Audio-visual texts are powerful precisely because they work on you faster than you can think about them — which is exactly why listening to them well is a skill, and not simply a matter of paying attention.

#### 2.1 Listening to an audio-visual text

An **audio-visual text** carries its meaning in three streams at once: the words, the pictures and the sound. They usually agree. When they do not, the disagreement is the most interesting thing in the text.

| **Stream** | **What it carries** | **What to note** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Spoken words | the claims, the figures, the attributionswho said it, and whether they were | there |
| Pictures (footage) | evidence of what a place looked like | when it was filmed, and what is outside the frame |
| Captions | names, places, dates, figures | captions often carry the only exact data |
| Music | how you are meant to feel | music under a news item is an argument |
| Sound bites | the voices selected to be heard | whose voice was chosen, and how long they were given |

*Table 2.1 — Five streams in an audio-visual text.*

##### 2.1.1 Taking notes while watching

Notes on a broadcast must be taken while it runs; there is no going back. Rule your page into three columns before it starts.

| **Said** | **Shown** | **My question** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Flooding worst in ten years — Assembly | water to knee height, one street | worst by what measure? |
| 1,200 households affected | market stalls under water | households or people? |
| Drains last cleared in 2022 | silted drain, close-up | cleared by whom? |
| “We shall act before the next rains” — DCE speaking indoors DCE |  | what exactly is *act*? |

*Table 2.2 — A three-column listening sheet.*

> **Exam tip 2.1**
>
> Write figures the instant you hear them, even mid-word. A sentence can be rebuilt from memory thirty seconds later; *one thousand two hundred households* cannot.

The third column is what makes this method worth the trouble. Questions written during listening are far better than questions thought up afterwards, because afterwards you only remember what you understood.

#### 2.2 Supporting what you say with the text

After listening comes the assessed part: using the text. Three levels of answer are possible, and the difference between them is what separates grades.

| **Level** | **What it sounds like** |
| --- | --- |
| Bare opinion | *The report was about a flood. It was sad.* |
| Opinion with recall | *The report said 1,200 households were affected and that* *the drains had not been cleared since 2022.* |
| Opinion supported by the text | *The Assembly called it the worst flooding in ten years, but* *the only figure given — 1,200 households — was not* *compared with any earlier year, so the claim was not* *actually supported in the report.* |

*Table 2.3 — Three levels of answer.*

**Use the speaker's own words** where they matter, and say whose they are: *the District Chief Executive*

- *said he would “act before the next rains”*. **Use the figures exactly**, with their units, and say what they were not compared with.

- **Use the pictures as evidence too**: *the drain shown in close-up was silted to about half its depth*.

- **Separate what the text showed from what it claimed.** A picture of one flooded street is not evidence

- about a district.

##### 2.2.1 Selection and bias

Every report leaves things out; there is no other way to make one. **Selection** becomes **bias** when the leaving-out runs consistently in one direction. Four questions expose it.

**Who speaks, and for how long?** Count the seconds if you can.

- **Whose voice is missing?** In a report on a flood: the people who built on the waterway, the engineer,

- the officer who signed the permits. **Is the commentary doing work the pictures cannot?** *The residents are angry* — over pictures of people

- standing quietly. **What is the music doing?** Slow strings under an interview tell you how to feel before you have decided.

> - **Common misconception 2.1**
>
> *If I saw it, it happened as shown.* Footage is true and partial at the same time. A camera shows one flooded street because that is where the road was passable; the dry streets are equally real and were not filmed. Ask always what is outside the frame — not because reports lie, but because a frame has edges.

>
> 1. Watch or listen to a news report twice, keeping a three-column sheet. Compare your column of figures with a partner's.
>

>

>
> 4. Find one place where the commentary claimed something the pictures did not show.
>
> 5. Explain the difference between selection and bias, with an example of each.

#### 2.3 Collaborative discussion

A **collaborative discussion** is one in which the group ends up somewhere none of its members started. It requires three things that competitive argument does not: initiating, building, and closing.

##### 2.3.1 Initiating

**Initiating** is opening a discussion rather than waiting to be asked. It is the single most valuable habit in group work and the rarest in a classroom.

| **Way of initiating** | **Example** |
| --- | --- |
| Put the question | *Before we start — are we discussing the flooding, or the* *drains?* |
| Propose a structure | *Shall we take causes first and remedies afterwards?* |
| Offer a position to be attacked | *I will start with a view I am not sure of, so that you can test* *it.* |
| Bring in the evidence | *The report gave one figure. Can we build round it?* |
| Bring in a person | *Efua lives near the river. Efua, start us off.* |

*Table 2.4 — Five ways of starting a discussion.*

##### 2.3.2 Building on others

| **Instead of** | **Say** |
| --- | --- |
| No, the real problem is the drains. | That is right about the rainfall, and I think the drains multiply it. |
| I disagree completely. | I follow the first half. It is the conclusion I cannot get to — may I say why? |
| As I was saying… | Kojo's point about permits changes something I said earlier. |
| That's what I said. | Efua has put it better than I did; can I add one thing to it? |
| Anyway… | Before we leave that — is there evidence either way? |

*Table 2.5 — Building instead of replacing.*

**Probing questions** deepen a discussion without opposing anybody: *What makes you say that? Has that happened here? What would we expect to see if that were true?* Each asks the speaker to go further rather than to defend themselves.

##### 2.3.3 Expressing your own view persuasively

**Say it in one sentence first.** *I think the permits are the point.* Then explain.

- **Give one reason, not four.** Four reasons dilute; one reason with evidence persuades.

- **Anticipate the obvious objection** and answer it before it is put.

- **Mark it as a view.** *In my view*, *it seems to me*, *I would argue* — so nobody has to guess which parts are

- fact. **Invite the test.** *Tell me what is wrong with that.* People who ask this are listened to more, not less.

> - **Dialogue 2.1 — A group discussion after a documentary on water**
>
> **Yaa:** Before we start — are we discussing the boreholes, or the river? They are not the same problem.
>
> **Kojo:** The river. The documentary was about the river.
>
> **Efua:** It was about both, actually. The last four minutes were the boreholes, and that is where the figure came from — sixty per cent working.
>
> **Yaa:** Then let us take the river first and come back to the sixty per cent. Kojo?
>
> **Kojo:** My view is that the pollution is the whole story. If the river were clean, nobody would need a borehole.

<!-- page 21 -->

> **Efua:** I follow that as far as the clean river. Where I cannot follow you is *nobody would need* — my grandmother's village is eleven kilometres from the river, and it was eleven kilometres before anyone polluted anything.
>
> **Kojo:** That is fair. Distance is a separate problem from pollution.
>
> **Yaa:** So we have two problems and the documentary treated them as one. Does anyone know whether the sixty per cent figure counted only boreholes, or standpipes as well?
>
> **Efua:** The caption said boreholes. The commentary said *water points*.
>
> **Yaa:** Then we should not use the figure until we know which. Can we agree on that?
>
> **Kojo:** Agreed.
>
> **Yaa:** Then where we have got to is this: the film ran together two different problems, pollution and distance;
>
> we accept both are real; and we cannot use its one figure until we know what it counted. Is that fair to everybody?

Yaa initiates, proposes a structure, brings a person in, tests a figure, and closes with a summary the whole group can accept. Efua builds twice — *I follow that as far as* — and produces the caption that catches the discrepancy. #### 3.1 /r/ and /l/

These two sounds are made close together in the mouth, and many languages have only one of them, so speakers of English across the world merge them at some point. The difference is worth mastering because English uses it to distinguish a great many pairs of words.

| **Sound** | **How it is made** | **Note** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| /l/ | tongue tip touches the ridge behind the teeth; air passes along the sides | a **lateral**; it can be held: *llllll* |
| /r/ | tongue tip curls towards the ridge but an **approximant**; the tongue never does not touch; no friction | makes contact |

*Table 3.1 — /l/ and /r/ compared.*

The test is contact. For /l/ the tongue **touches**; for /r/ it does not. Say *la-la-la* and feel the tap; say *ra-ra-ra* and feel that nothing lands.

##### 3.1.1 The three positions

| **Position** | **/r/** | **/l/** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Initial | *rain, road, right, run, river* | *lane, load, light, love, lesson* |
| Medial | *arrive, carry, sorry, borrow, forest* | *follow, silly, believe, collect, salad* |
| Final | *water, better, teacher, tailor, matter* | *school, table, people, careful, hospital* |
| In a cluster | *bread, train, from, grow, print* | *black, please, clean, flat, glass* |

*Table 3.2 — /r/ and /l/ in four positions.*

Final /l/ is the position that gives most trouble. In *school*, *table* and *hospital* the tongue must still reach the ridge at the very end of the word, when the mouth is already relaxing. Practise by saying the word, holding the final *l* for two seconds, and then shortening it.

| **/r/ word** | **/l/ word** | **Test sentence** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| right | light | Turn *right* at the *light*. |
| rice | lice | The *rice* is clean. |
| road | load | The *load* fell on the *road*. |
| fry | fly | Do not *fry* the *fly*. |
| pray | play | They *pray* before they *play*. |
| arrive | alive | We *arrive* *alive*. |
| correct | collect | *Collect* the *correct* forms. |
| grass | glass | *Glass* in the *grass*. |

*Table 3.3 — Minimal pairs: /r/ and /l/.*

#### 3.2 Consonant clusters

A **consonant cluster** is two or more consonants together with no vowel between them. English allows up to three at the beginning of a word and up to four at the end — more than most languages, which is why clusters are the commonest place for a learner's pronunciation to give way.

| **Cluster** | **Where** | **Examples** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| two at the start | initial | *play, stop, from, skin, twin, dry* |
| three at the start | initial | *street, spring, splash, scratch, strong* |
| two at the end | final | *hand, jump, ask, films, lift* |
| three at the end | final | *hands, jumped, asked, texts, world* |
| four at the end | final | *strengths, twelfths, glimpsed, prompts* |

*Table 3.4 — Clusters of two, three and four.*

##### 3.2.1 The two things that go wrong

**Vowel insertion.** A vowel is slipped inside the cluster to make it easier: *sukul* for *school*, *pirinting* for

- *printing*, *filim* for *film*. The remedy is to say the two consonants slowly as one movement, then speed up without letting the vowel back in. **Cluster reduction.** A consonant is dropped from the end: *jump* for *jumped*, *tes* for *tests*, *ol* for *old*. This

- matters more than it sounds, because the dropped consonant is usually the one carrying the grammar.

| **Written** | **Cluster** | **What the ending carries** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *asked* | /skt/ | past tense |
| *jumped* | /mpt/ | past tense |
| *hands* | /ndz/ | plural |
| *texts* | /ksts/ | plural |
| *world's* | /ldz/ | possession |
| *strengths* | /ŋkθs/ | plural |

*Table 3.5 — Final clusters that carry grammar.*

Native speakers simplify some of these too — few people sound every consonant of *texts* in rapid speech. What is not acceptable in writing or in an oral examination is losing the ending altogether, so that *he asked for his books yesterday* becomes *he ask for his book yesterday* and the sentence loses both its tense and its plural.

#### 3.3 Word stress

A **syllable** is a unit of speech built round one vowel sound. **Stress** is the extra force given to one syllable, and it is made of three things at once: the syllable is louder, longer and higher in pitch. In this book a stressed syllable is shown in capitals: *TEAcher*, *aBOUT*.

| **Word** | **Syllables** | **Stress** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *school* | 1 — monosyllabic | no choice; the word is one beat |
| *teacher* | 2 — disyllabic | TEAcher |
| *about* | 2 | aBOUT |
| *water* | 2 | WAter |
| *begin* | 2 | beGIN |
| *market* | 2 | MARket |
| *forget* | 2 | forGET |

*Table 3.6 — Stress in one- and two-syllable words.*

##### 3.3.1 A useful tendency

English is irregular, but a tendency exists and it is worth knowing: two-syllable **nouns and adjectives** are more often stressed on the first syllable, while two-syllable **verbs** are more often stressed on the second.

| **Nouns and adjectives (first)** | **Verbs (second)** |
| --- | --- |
| TAble, MARket, TEAcher, MOther | beGIN, forGET, aRRIVE, deCIDE |
| HAPPY, CLEver, QUIet, YELLow | aGREE, exPLAIN, resPOND, comPLAIN |
| PAper, WINdow, PROblem, WAter | atTACK, preFER, disCUSS, aLLOW |

*Table 3.7 — The tendency in two-syllable words.*

The tendency has exceptions — *reply* is a noun stressed on the second syllable, *promise* a verb stressed on the first — so it is a starting point rather than a rule. But it explains the most useful pattern in the language: the noun–verb pairs.

##### 3.3.2 Stress shift: the same word, two classes

| --- | --- | --- |

| present | a PREsent for my sister | he will preSENT the report |
| object | the OBject of the exercise | I obJECT to that |
| conduct | her CONduct was praised | they will conDUCT a survey |
| increase | an INcrease in fees | fees will inCREASE |
| conflict | a CONflict of interest | the accounts conFLICT |
| export | our chief EXport | we exPORT cocoa |
| contract | sign the CONtract | metal will conTRACT |
| permit | a driving PERmit | will you perMIT it? |
| rebel | a REbel army | they reBEL against it |

*Table 3.8 — Ten noun–verb pairs distinguished only by stress.*

##### 3.3.3 Sentence stress

Within a sentence, one word usually carries the main force, and moving it changes the meaning entirely — without changing a single word.

| **Stressed word** | **Meaning** |
| --- | --- |
| **I** did not say she took the money. | somebody else said it |
| I **did not** say she took the money. | I deny saying it |
| I did not **say** she took the money. | I implied it, perhaps |
| I did not say **she** took the money. | somebody else took it |
| I did not say she **took** the money. | she did something else with it |
| I did not say she took the **money**. | she took something else |

*Table 3.9 — One sentence, six meanings.*

#### Chapter summary

- For /l/ the tongue touches the ridge; for /r/ it approaches but never lands.

- Both sounds must be produced in initial, medial and final position, and final /l/ — *school*, *table* — is the hardest.

- A consonant cluster is two or more consonants with no vowel between; English allows three at the start and four at the end.

- Vowel insertion (*sukul*) and cluster reduction (*jump* for *jumped*) are the two faults to guard against.

- The consonant lost at the end of a cluster is usually the one carrying the tense, the plural or the possessive.

- A stressed syllable is louder, longer and higher in pitch than the others.

- Two-syllable nouns and adjectives tend to be stressed on the first syllable, verbs on the second.

- Ten common words are distinguished as noun or verb by stress alone: REcord and reCORD, PERmit and perMIT.

- Moving the stress within a sentence changes its meaning without changing a word.

- Reading aloud is assessed for clarity and accuracy, not for an imitated accent.

#### Engage

#### 4.3 Evaluating reasoning

To evaluate an argument, separate its steps. What is the conclusion? What reason is given for it? What evidence supports the reason? Then ask whether the step from each to the next actually holds.

| **Fallacy** | **What it does** | **Example** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hasty generalisation | concludes from too few cases | *Two learners cheated; this class is* *dishonest.* |
| False cause | assumes that what came first caused what followed | *Since the mast was built, the rains* *have failed.* |
| False dilemma | offers two options as though there were no others | *Either we ban sand winning or we lose* *the river.* |
| Appeal to popularity | argues from how many believe it | *Everybody knows the scheme is a* *waste.* |
| Appeal to authority | cites an authority outside its field | *A footballer says the drug is safe.* |
| Circular reasoning | uses the conclusion as its own reason | *It is illegal because it is not allowed.* |
| Ad hominem | attacks the person instead of the argument | *He would say that; he owns a lorry.* |
| Slippery slope | assumes one step leads inevitably to an extreme | *Allow phones and no one will ever* *read again.* |

*Table 4.3 — Eight faults in reasoning.*

> **Model 4.1 — Evaluating the editorial**
>
> **Its conclusion:** the by-law as passed will not be enforceable.
>
> **Its reason:** it restricts an activity without knowing how many people depend on it, and offers a register rather than an alternative.
>
> **Its evidence:** Mr Asare's own admission, reported in Passage 4.1, that the figure is not known; and the comparison with the fishing closed season.
>
> **Does the step hold?** Partly. The admission is solid evidence, and it comes from the Assembly's own officer, which makes it strong. The fishing comparison is weaker: one case is not a pattern, and the editorial does not show that the two situations are alike in the respects that matter. It is close to a **hasty generalisation** — though it is offered as an illustration rather than as proof, which is a fair use of it.
>
> **What it omits:** the Water Resources Commission's advice, mentioned in the report. An editorial arguing that a by-law is badly made should say why the expert advice behind it was wrong, and this one does not mention that there was any.

#### 4.4 Credibility and importance

**Credibility** asks how far a source deserves to be believed. **Importance** asks whether a piece of information matters to the question in hand. They are different tests, and a reader needs both.

| **Question** | **What it tells you** |
| --- | --- |
| Who is saying it? | expertise, position, and whether they were present |
| How do they know? | first hand, second hand, or supposition |
| When was it said? | how much may have changed since |
| What do they stand to gain or lose? | a **vested interest** does not make a source wrong, but it must be weighed |
| Is it corroborated? | does an independent source say the same? |
| Is the method given? | a figure without a method cannot be checked |
| What is left out? | the strongest signal of all |

*Table 4.4 — Seven credibility questions.*

**A source with an interest may still be right.** The Assembly's officer has an interest in the by-law

- succeeding, and his figure about the riverbed may be perfectly accurate. Weigh it; do not discard it. **Corroboration beats confidence.** One cautious source confirmed by another is worth more than a

- single certain one. **Distinguish important from interesting.** That one member abstained is interesting. That nobody knows

- how many workers are affected is important, because the whole argument turns on it.

> **Common misconception 4.1**
>
> *If two sources disagree, the truth is in the middle.* Sometimes; often not. One source may simply be wrong, or better informed, or measuring a different thing. Ask which of them was present, which gives a method, and which can be checked — and be willing to conclude that one is right and the other mistaken.

#### 4.5 How the media spreads information

The same information reaches people through several channels, and each changes it. Judging a claim means knowing which channel it came down.

| **Channel** | **Strength** | **Weakness** |
| --- | --- | --- |

| Television | shows what a place looks like | shows only what a camera reached |
| Newspaper | detail, attribution, a record you can return to | a day late; reaches those who buy it |
| Notice board and durbar | authoritative and local | reaches only those present |
| Social media | instant, and everybody can publish | no editor; a claim spreads faster than its correction |
| Word of mouth | trusted, and it reaches the | changes at every telling |

#### Chapter summary

- Different text types do different jobs: a report informs, an editorial argues, a press release defends.

- An editorial is evidence about the newspaper, not about the world.

- Two texts on one event may share every fact and differ entirely in reasoning — and reasoning can be evaluated.

- Separate an argument into conclusion, reason and evidence, then test whether each step holds.

- Hasty generalisation, false cause, false dilemma, appeal to popularity and circular reasoning are the faults most often met.

- Credibility asks whether a source deserves belief; importance asks whether the information matters to the question.

- A source with a vested interest may still be right — weigh it rather than discard it.

- When two sources disagree, the truth is not automatically in the middle.

- A claim appearing on six channels may be one source repeated: ask where it started and how that person knew.

#### Engage

There is a kind of reading you have done all your life — following a story to find out what happens. #### 5.1 Reading against the clock

**Scanning** is searching for one thing while deliberately not reading anything else. It is the fastest reading skill there is, and it must be practised against a clock or it will not be there when a clock appears.

**Know the shape of what you are looking for.** A year is four digits; a name has a capital; a quantity has a

- unit. Look for the shape, not the meaning. **Use the text features.** Headings, bold terms, the first word of each paragraph.

- **Move your eye down, not across.** Full reading goes along the line; scanning goes down the page.

- **Stop when you find it.** Reading on *to be sure* costs the time you saved.

#### 5.3 A complex passage

> **Passage 5.1 — The river that moved**
>
> There was a river behind my grandmother's compound, and for the first ten years of my life I believed it had always been where it was. Rivers, I thought, were like hills: they were the part of the world that stayed still while people came and went across them.
>
> She corrected me, without meaning to, in the middle of an ordinary afternoon. She was pointing out where her mother's house had stood, and her finger went out over the water, thirty metres from the bank, and stopped in the middle of the current. *There,* she said. *The kitchen was about where that log is.* Then she went back to picking stones out of the rice, and I stood looking at a stretch of moving water where a kitchen had been, and understood for the first time that the river was not a thing but an event — something that was happening, slowly, and had been happening the whole time I had been watching it and thinking it still.
>
> A river takes what it is given. When the trees along the upper slopes were cut in the years before I was born, the rain that used to be held began to arrive all at once instead, carrying the soil with it. Water heavy with sediment cuts more deeply than clear water; a deeper channel carries more water; more water cuts more deeply still. Nobody upstream did anything dramatic. They cut trees, one household at a time, for perfectly good reasons, and none of them saw a kitchen fall in.
>
> That is the difficulty with damage of this kind: it has no author. A flood has an author — the rains of a particular week, which everybody can name and complain about. Erosion has only arithmetic. Each household's contribution is too small to be worth regulating, and the sum of them is too large to be repaired, and between the two there is a space in which nothing is anybody's fault and everything is somebody's loss.
>
> My grandmother, who did not use words like *sediment*, put it another way. She said the river was a patient creditor. It had lent the family a kitchen for forty years and had come, without raising its voice, to collect.
>
> I have thought about that sentence for a long time. It is not true, of course; rivers lend nothing and collect nothing. But it does something no accurate sentence I can construct manages to do. It makes the loss *ordinary* — a debt falling due, the most ordinary thing in a trading town — and in doing so it removes the one comfort that would otherwise be available to us, which is the belief that what happened was exceptional. It was not exceptional. It is still going on. Somewhere upstream this afternoon, somebody is cutting a tree for a perfectly good reason.

#### 5.4 Complex questions and their answers

A complex question cannot be answered by copying. It asks you to gather, weigh, infer or explain. The method is the same each time: restate the key words of the question, answer in a sentence, then cite the text.

| **Question type** | **Example on Passage 5.1** | **What the answer must contain** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Gather | State three stages by which the erosion worsened. | three separate points, in order |
| Infer | What does the writer's use of *event* rather than *thing* suggest? | an interpretation plus the words that carry it |
| Weigh | Whose account explains more — the grandmother's or the writer's? | a position, and a reason for it |
| Explain effect | Why is the final sentence disturbing? | what it does to the reader, and how |
| Vocabulary in use | What does *sediment* mean as used here? | the sense in this passage, not the dictionary's first sense |

*Table 5.3 — Five complex question types.*

> **Model 5.1 — A weak answer and a strong one**
>
> **Question:** *Explain the effect of the last sentence of the passage, and how the writer achieves it.*
>
> **Weak:** *The last sentence is very effective. It makes the reader think about the environment and it is a good way to end the passage.*
>
> **Strong:** *The last sentence — “Somewhere upstream this afternoon, somebody is cutting a tree for a perfectly good reason” — moves the whole account out of the past and into the present tense, so that the reader cannot treat it as something that has finished. Three things produce the effect. The vagueness of “somewhere” and “somebody” means the reader cannot picture a culprit to be angry with. The phrase “a perfectly good reason” repeats, word for word, the phrase used earlier about the writer's own neighbours, so no one is being blamed.*
>
> *And “this afternoon” places the event inside the reader's own day. The effect is not indignation but unease, which is harder to put down.*
>
> **What changed:** the strong answer quotes exactly, names the effect precisely — *unease*, not *effective* — and identifies the three devices producing it. Notice that *it is a good way to end the passage* says nothing at all.

#### Engage

Summarising a paragraph is a school exercise. Summarising a whole argument fairly — including the parts you dislike — is one of the hardest things an educated adult does, and one of the most useful. Anyone who can state an opponent's case so well that the opponent says *yes, that is what I mean* has earned the right to disagree with it. Anyone who cannot has not yet understood what they are arguing against.

#### 6.1 Analysing a whole text

Analysing a paragraph asks what it says. Analysing a **whole text** asks what each part is *for* and how the parts hold together. Five questions do it.

**What is the central idea?** One sentence, in your own words. If you cannot write it, you have not

- finished reading. **What is each paragraph doing?** Not what it says — what job it performs: opening, defining, giving

- evidence, conceding, objecting, illustrating, concluding. **Where is the evidence, and how good is it?** Figures, cases, authorities, or none.

- **What does the text assume?** Every argument rests on something it does not argue for.

- **Where does it turn?** Most arguments have one point at which they change direction — *but*, *however*,

- *the difficulty is*.

> **Passage 6.1 — Should the school day start later?**
>
> **1** Every school in this district opens at seven-thirty. Nobody chose that time. It is inherited, like the length of the term and the colour of the uniform, and like most inherited things it is defended chiefly on the ground that it has always been so.
>
> **2** There is a case for changing it. Adolescents do not fall asleep at the hour younger children do; their bodies shift later during those years, and a learner who cannot sleep before eleven and must rise at half past five is not lazy but short of sleep. Schools abroad that have moved their opening time by an hour report better attendance and fewer late arrivals.
>
> **3** The case against is practical and it is serious. Parents leave for work at six. A learner who need not be at school until half past eight is a learner alone in the house, or a learner at the roadside for an hour. In a district where most pupils walk, a later start also means a later finish, and walking home at six in the evening in the rainy season is not a small thing to ask.
>
> **4** There is also the question of what the extra hour would be used for. The assumption behind the proposal is that learners would sleep it. Some would. Others would spend it exactly as they spend the hour before eleven at night, and arrive no better rested and an hour later.
>
> **5** But the strongest objection is one that the proposal's supporters rarely address. The argument rests on studies of learners who are driven to school and who go to bed when they choose. A learner here who fetches water at five is not short of sleep because of an hour of biology; she is short of sleep because of an hour of work. Move the school day and the water remains where it is. You would have relieved the wrong hour.

<!-- page 53 -->

> **6** None of this makes the proposal wrong. It makes it partial. A district that wanted its learners rested would look first at what happens to them between five and seven in the morning, and only then at what time the bell rings.

| **Paragraph** | **What it does** | **Evidence in it** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | opens; frames the current time as unexamined habit | none — and none needed |
| 2 | states the case for | biology of adolescent sleep; schools abroad |
| 3 | states the practical case against | local conditions: work hours, walking, rains |
| 4 | names an assumption in the proposal | none; a reasoned doubt |
| 5 | the turn: the strongest objection | the studies' population differs from this one |
| 6 | concludes: not wrong but partial | restates the priority |

*Table 6.1 — The function of each paragraph of Passage 6.1.*

Notice paragraph 5. A reader who summarised this text as *the writer is against a later start* would be wrong: paragraph 6 says explicitly that the proposal is not wrong but **partial**. Summarising a text means catching that distinction, not the general direction of the writer's feeling.

#### 6.2 Assumptions

An **assumption** is something a text takes for granted. Assumptions are not faults — every argument has them — but an argument stands or falls on whether its assumptions hold in the case at hand.

| **The claim** | **What it assumes** |
| --- | --- |
| A later start would rest learners | that the freed hour would be slept |
| Studies abroad apply here | that the learners studied live as ours do |
| Attendance would improve | that lateness is caused by sleep, not by distance |
| Parents could adjust | that work start times are negotiable |
| The school controls the outcome | that nothing else in the morning competes with sleep |

*Table 6.2 — Five assumptions inside one proposal.*

> **Exam tip 6.1**
>
> A question of the form *what does the writer assume?* is answered with a full sentence beginning *The writer assumes that…*, followed by the words in the text that reveal it. An assumption is by definition unstated, so you must cite what implies it, not what says it.

#### 6.3 Summarising a whole text

The three rules of an objective summary are unchanged from Basic 8: add nothing, omit nothing important, keep the proportions. At Basic 9 a fourth is added: **the summary must be recognisable to the writer**.

**Central idea first.** One sentence carrying the whole position, including its qualifications.

- **One line per section**, in the original's order.

- **Generic terms** for lists; **paraphrase**, not lifting.

- **Keep the concessions.** A writer who admitted something must be shown admitting it — this is the

- commonest failure in summaries of arguments.

<!-- page 54 -->

**No opinion, no example of your own, no conclusion the writer did not draw.**

- **Count the words** and write the number.

> - **Model 6.1 — Summarising Passage 6.1 in about ninety words**
>
> **Weak (a summary of the reader, not the text):** *This passage is about school times. The writer says schools abroad start later and it helps. But in Ghana it would not work because of parents and rain. I agree with the writer that we should not change the time because our situation is different and pupils have work to do at home.*
>
> **Strong (objective, proportioned, recognisable):** *School opening times in the district are inherited rather than chosen. The writer accepts the case for a later start — adolescents sleep later, and schools abroad that have moved their opening report better attendance — but sets against it three difficulties: learners would be unsupervised or on the road, a later finish means walking home at dusk, and the freed hour might not be slept.*
>
> *The strongest objection is that the supporting studies come from learners who do no morning work. The writer concludes that the proposal is not wrong but partial, and that the hours before school should be examined first.*
>
> **What changed:** the weak version reverses the writer's conclusion (*we should not change the time*) and adds the summariser's own agreement. The strong version keeps the concession — *the writer accepts the case for* — reproduces the order, and ends where the text ends, on *partial* rather than *wrong*.

> **Common misconception 6.1**
>
> *A summary should say whether the argument is any good.* No. That is an evaluation, and it is a separate task, usually worth separate marks. Mixing the two produces a summary nobody can trust and an evaluation nobody can check. Summarise first, completely; then, under its own heading, say what you think.

#### 6.4 Summarising what you disagree with

The hardest summary is of a text whose position you reject. Four leaks let the disagreement show, and examiners see all four.

| **The leak** | **How it shows** | **The repair** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Loaded reporting verbs | *the writer claims*, *alleges*, *insists* | use *states*, *argues*, *says* |
| Weakening qualifiers | *the writer merely says*, *only offers* | remove them |
| Selective omission | the writer's best point is missing | check every section is represented |
| Adding the answer | *though this ignores the cost* | delete; it belongs in the evaluation |

*Table 6.3 — Four ways a summary betrays its writer.*

The test is simple and severe: would the writer of the original read your summary and say *yes, that is what I mean*? If not, it is not yet a summary.

>

>

>

>

>

#### Engage

*The tall man in the blue overall who fixed our pump last year* is twelve words long and it is grammatically one thing: a noun. Everything after *man* is there to narrow down which man. English builds meaning by packing modifiers round a head word, and a writer who can do this deliberately says in one sentence what a weaker writer takes four to say. This chapter is about packing and unpacking.

#### 7.1 Noun phrases

A **noun phrase** is a group of words doing the work of a noun. At its centre is the **head word**; everything else modifies it. Test any group by replacing it with *it* or *they*: if the sentence still works, the group is a noun phrase.

> **Common misconception 7.1**
>
> *Few and a few mean the same.* They are almost opposites in effect. **A few** is positive — *we have a few options* means there are some. **Few** is negative — *we have few options* means hardly any. The same holds for **a little** (some) and **little** (hardly any). *She has little hope* and *she has a little hope* would be reported very differently by a newspaper.

Two further points that examinations test. Some nouns that are countable in Ghanaian usage are uncountable in standard written English: *information*, *advice*, *equipment*, *furniture*, *luggage*, *machinery*, *staff*. Write *a piece of information*, *some advice*, *two items of equipment* — not *informations* or *an advice*. And keep **fewer** for countables and **less** for uncountables: *fewer pupils*, *less water*.

#### 7.3 Phrasal verbs

A **phrasal verb** is a verb plus one or two particles whose meaning cannot be worked out from the verb alone. *Look* means one thing; *look after*, *look up*, *look into*, *look down on* and *look forward to* mean five others.

| **Phrasal verb** | **Meaning** | **Example** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| call off | cancel | *The match was called off.* |
| put up with | tolerate | *We put up with the noise for a year.* |
| look into | investigate | *The assembly will look into the* *complaint.* |
| carry out | perform | *They carried out a survey.* |
| turn down | reject | *The bank turned down her application.* |
| come across | meet or find by chance | *I came across the file yesterday.* |
| run into | meet unexpectedly; collide with | *He ran into his old teacher.* |
| pick up | collect; learn informally | *She picked up Ewe in two years.* |
| give in | surrender | *Neither side would give in.* |
| do away with | abolish | *They did away with the fee.* |
| get on with | have a good relationship with; continue | *They get on with their neighbours.* |
| bring about | cause | *The rains brought about the collapse.* |

*Table 7.6 — Twelve phrasal verbs of two and three parts.*

##### 7.3.1 Separable and inseparable

**Separable.** The object may stand between the verb and the particle: *call the match off* or *call off the*

- *match*. When the object is a pronoun it **must** go in the middle: *call it off*, never *call off it*. **Inseparable.** The parts stay together: *look into the complaint*, never *look the complaint into*. Three-part

- phrasal verbs — *put up with*, *get on with*, *do away with* — are always inseparable.

| **Correct** | **Wrong** |
| --- | --- |
| Turn the radio down. / Turn down the radio. | — |
| Turn it down. | Turn down it. |
| We looked into the matter. | We looked the matter into. |
| They put up with him. | They put him up with. |
| She picked it up quickly. | She picked up it quickly. |

*Table 7.7 — Separable and inseparable in use.*

>

> *investigate* for *look into*, *cancel* for *call off*, *tolerate* for *put up with*, *abolish* for *do away with*. Knowing both, and choosing, is what is being assessed.

#### 7.4 Adverbial phrases

An **adverbial phrase** answers one of five questions about the verb: how, when, where, why or how often. It may be a prepositional phrase, an adverb with its modifiers, or an infinitive of purpose.

| **Question** | **Kind** | **Example** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| How? | manner | *He answered with great care.* |
| When? | time | **\*Early on Friday morning**, the tanker arrived.\* |
| Where? | place | *They waited at the far end of the* *compound.* |
| Why? | purpose or reason | *She left early to catch the last lorry.* |
| How often? | frequency | *The committee meets twice a term.* |
| To what degree? | degree | *The tank was almost completely* *empty.* |

*Table 7.8 — Six kinds of adverbial phrase.*

##### 7.4.1 Position and emphasis

| **Position** | **Example** | **Effect** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Front | *In the third week of the dry season, the* sets the scene; the reader is placed *pump stopped.* | before the event |
| Middle | *The pump, in the third week of the dry* interrupts; use sparingly *season, stopped.* |  |
| End | *The pump stopped in the third week of* plainest; the event comes first *the dry season.* |  |

*Table 7.9 — Three positions, three effects.*

#### Chapter summary

- A noun phrase does the work of a noun; test it by replacing the whole group with *it* or *they*.

- A noun phrase has four slots: determiner, pre-modifiers, head, post-modifiers.

- A noun phrase can be subject, object, complement, or the object of a preposition.

- Adjectives before a head follow a fixed order, and three is the practical limit.

- *Many, few* and *fewer* go with countables; *much, little* and *less* go with uncountables.

<!-- page 64 -->

- *A few* and *a little* mean some; *few* and *little* mean hardly any — nearly the opposite.

- Information, advice, equipment, furniture, luggage and staff are uncountable in standard written English.

- A phrasal verb's meaning cannot be worked out from the verb alone, and a pronoun object must sit inside a separable one: *turn it down*.

- Three-part phrasal verbs are always inseparable, and all phrasal verbs are informal by nature.

- An adverbial phrase answers how, when, where, why, how often or to what degree, and fronting it varies the rhythm — provided it modifies what you intend.

#### 8.1 Conditional sentences

A **conditional sentence** has two parts: the **if-clause** stating a condition, and the **main clause** stating what follows. English has three main patterns, and they differ in how likely the condition is.

| **Type** | **Pattern** | **Example** | **What it means** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Zero | if + present, present | *If you heat water, it boils.* | always true |
| First | if + present, will/shall + verb*If it rains, we shall stay* | *indoors.* | a real future possibility |
| Second | if + past, would + verb | *If I had a bicycle, I would* *ride to school.* | unreal or unlikely now |
| Third | if + past perfect, would have *If we had left earlier, we* + past participle | *would have caught the* *lorry.* | impossible: the past is fixed |

*Table 8.1 — Four conditional patterns.*

##### 8.1.1 The third conditional

The **third conditional** is the Basic 9 structure. It speaks of a past condition that did not happen and the result that therefore did not follow. Every third-conditional sentence carries two facts inside it, both negative.

| **Sentence** | **Fact 1** | **Fact 2** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| If we had left earlier, we would have caught the lorry. | we did not leave earlier | we did not catch it |
| If the drains had been cleared, the market would not have flooded. | they were not cleared | it flooded |
| If she had not fetched water, she would have been on time. | she fetched water | she was late |
| If they had counted the workers, the by-law would have been enforceable. | they did not count them | it was not enforceable |

<!-- page 68 -->

*Table 8.2 — Two facts inside every third conditional.*

**The form is fixed.** *If* + **had** + past participle, then **would have** + past participle. *Would* never appears in

- the if-clause: *if I would have known* is wrong; *if I had known* is right. **Other modals may replace \*would** *in the main clause to change the strength:* could have *(would have*

- *been able),* might have\* (possibly). **The clauses may be reversed.** *We would have caught the lorry if we had left earlier.* When the if-clause

- comes first, a comma separates them; when it comes second, no comma is needed. **Mixed conditional.** A past condition may have a present result: *If they had counted the workers, we*

- *would know the number today.* Past perfect in the if-clause, *would* + verb in the main clause.

> **Common misconception 8.1**
>
> *If I would have known, I would have come.* This is the commonest error in the third conditional and it is heard constantly. **Would** belongs only in the main clause. The if-clause takes *had*: *If I had known, I would have come.*

#### 8.2 Defining and non-defining relative clauses

A **defining** relative clause tells the reader *which* one is meant and takes no commas. A **non-defining** clause adds information about something already identified and is fenced with commas. The commas carry meaning, so they are not optional.

| **Sentence** | **Type** | **Meaning** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The learners who had paid were admitted. | defining | only those who had paid |
| The learners, who had paid, were admitted. | non-defining | all of them; incidentally they had paid |
| My sister who lives in Tamale is a nurse. | defining | I have more than one sister |
| My sister, who lives in Tamale, is a nurse. | non-defining | I have one sister |
| The pump that serves three schools has failed. | defining | that particular pump |
| The pump, which serves three schools, non-defining has failed. |  | there is one pump; here is a fact about it |

*Table 8.3 — The commas that change the meaning.*

**\*That\* cannot introduce a non-defining clause.** After a comma, use *who* or *which*.

- **The relative pronoun may be omitted in a defining clause when it is the object**: *the book (that) you*

- *lent me*. It may never be omitted when it is the subject: *the man who repaired it*.

**A non-defining clause may refer to a whole idea**: *She arrived on time, which surprised everybody.*

- **Whose** works for people and things alike: *the school whose roof blew off*.

#### 8.3 Subject and predicate

Every sentence divides into two parts. The **subject** is what the sentence is about; the **predicate** is everything said about it, and it always contains the verb.

| **Subject** | **Predicate** |
| --- | --- |
| The tall man in the blue overall | repaired the pump on Friday. |
| Three farmers from the riverside villages | objected to the by-law. |
| What the assembly decided on Tuesday | surprised nobody. |
| Fetching water before school | costs her an hour every morning. |
| She | was late. |

*Table 8.4 — Subject and predicate.*

Find the subject by asking *who or what* before the verb. Note the last two rows: a subject need not be a simple noun — it may be a clause (*what the assembly decided*) or a phrase built on an *-ing* form (*fetching water before school*).

#### 8.4 Active and passive voice

**Voice** tells you whether the subject acts or is acted upon. In the **active** voice the subject does the action; in the **passive** the subject receives it.

| **Active** | **Passive** |
| --- | --- |
| The assembly approved the by-law. | The by-law was approved by the assembly. |
| Somebody has cleared the drains. | The drains have been cleared. |
| They will announce the results on Friday. | The results will be announced on Friday. |
| The head is signing the forms. | The forms are being signed by the head. |
| Nobody had counted the workers. | The workers had not been counted. |

*Table 8.6 — Active and passive.*

##### 8.4.1 Making the change

**The object of the active becomes the subject of the passive.**

- **The verb becomes \*be\* in the same tense, plus the past participle.**

- **The old subject becomes the agent, introduced by \*by\* — or disappears.**

- **Only verbs that take an object can be made passive.** *She arrived* has no passive.

##### 8.4.2 Choosing the passive deliberately

| **Use the passive when** | **Example** |
| --- | --- |
| The doer is unknown | *The window was broken during the night.* |
| The doer is obvious or unimportant | *The results will be announced on Friday.* |
| The receiver is the real subject of the paragraph | *The by-law was passed after two hours of debate.* |
| You want an impersonal, formal tone | *Applications are to be submitted by 30 June.* |
| You are writing a method or a procedure | *The water was tested at three points.* |

*Table 8.7 — Five good reasons for the passive.*

And one bad reason, which is worth knowing about because it will be used on you. The **agentless passive** removes the doer entirely: *mistakes were made*, *the funds were committed elsewhere*, *the drugs were allowed to expire*. Each sentence reports an event while declining to say who caused it. When you meet one in a report, the useful question is always the same: **by whom?**

> **Common misconception 8.2**
>
> *The passive voice is bad English and should always be avoided.* This advice, endlessly repeated, is wrong. The passive is the right choice whenever the receiver matters more than the doer, and it is standard in scientific and official writing. What deserves suspicion is not the passive but the missing agent — and only when the agent is being withheld rather than merely being obvious.

#### Chapter summary

- The four conditional patterns run from always true, through real future and unreal present, to the impossible past.

- The third conditional is *if* + had + past participle, then *would have* + past participle — and *would* never appears in the if-clause.

- Every third conditional carries two negative facts inside it: the condition did not happen and the result did not follow.

- A mixed conditional links a past condition to a present result.

- A defining relative clause takes no commas; a non-defining one is fenced with them, and *that* may not follow a comma.

- A relative pronoun may be dropped when it is the object of a defining clause, never when it is the subject.

- The subject is what a sentence is about; the predicate is everything said about it.

- The verb agrees with the simple subject, not with the nearest noun: *the list of names was posted*.

- The passive is right whenever the receiver matters more than the doer, and is standard in official and scientific writing.

- What deserves suspicion is not the passive but the missing agent: ask *by whom?*

#### Key words

> **Dash —** the long mark ( — ) marking an interruption, an explanation or a list.
>
> **Pair of dashes —** two dashes fencing an interruption within a sentence.
>
> **Hyphen —** the short mark ( - ) joining parts of a word.
>
> **Bracket —** the paired marks ( ) enclosing secondary material; also called parentheses.
>
> **Interruption —** a remark inserted into a sentence and not part of its grammar.
>
> **Compound word —** a word made of two or more words, such as \*father-in-law\*.
>
> **Attributive compound —** two or more words used together before a noun to describe it.
>
> **Prefix —** a form added to the front of a root word.
>
> **Root word —** the base word to which affixes are added.
>
> **Syllable break —** the point at which a word may be divided at the end of a line.
>
> **Cross reference —** a direction to another place in the same text.
>
> **Aside —** a remark that steps away from the main line of a sentence.
>
> **Ambiguity —** the state of having more than one possible meaning.
>
> **Parenthetical —** enclosed as an aside, between commas, dashes or brackets.
>
> **Suspended hyphen —** a hyphen left standing where a word is understood, as in \*two- and three-year courses\*.

#### Engage

<!-- page 76 -->

*A poor-rate collection* and *a poor rate-collection* are made of the same five words. The first is a collection of the poor rate; the second is a badly organised collection of rates. One short mark, moved one space, changes what the sentence is about. Commas and full stops you have had since Primary school. These three marks are the ones that separate a careful writer from a hurried one — and one of them can cost a contract.

#### 9.1 The dash

A **dash** is long. It separates parts of a sentence. A **hyphen** is short, and it joins parts of a word. They are not the same mark used in two ways; they are two marks, and mixing them is the commonest fault in this chapter.

##### 9.1.1 A pair of dashes: marking an interruption

Two dashes fence a remark that interrupts the sentence without belonging to its grammar. Remove the dashes and everything between them, and the sentence must still stand.

| **Sentence** | **Remove the interruption** |
| --- | --- |
| My son — where has he gone? — will be happy to see you. | My son will be happy to see you. |
| The engineer — a careful man in most things — had not counted the workers. | The engineer had not counted the workers. |
| The rains came late — as they now do most years — and the maize failed. | The rains came late and the maize failed. |

*Table 9.1 — A pair of dashes marking an interruption.*

Commas, dashes and brackets can all fence an interruption, and the choice tells the reader how far the remark stands from the sentence: **commas** keep it closest, **dashes** hold it at arm's length and give it emphasis, **brackets** push it furthest away and lower its importance.

| **Marks** | **Effect** | **Example** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Commas | part of the flow | The engineer, a careful man, had not counted the workers. |
| Dashes | a dramatic aside; emphasised | The engineer — a careful man in most things — had not counted the workers. |
| Brackets | an aside of lesser importance | The engineer (appointed in March) had not counted the workers. |

*Table 9.2 — Three ways of fencing an aside.*

##### 9.1.2 A single dash: explanation and list

**To introduce an explanation** of something just mentioned: *It was not a lion — it was a tiger — furiously*

- *lashing its way through the undergrowth.* **To introduce a list**: *Everyone needs good qualities — loyalty, discipline and honesty.*

- **To deliver a sting at the end of a sentence**: *The committee met, deliberated and resolved to appoint a*

- *sub-committee — its third this year.*

The colon does two of these jobs too. The difference is one of tone: a colon is neutral and formal, a dash is abrupt and informal. In a business letter, use the colon. In an article or a narrative, the dash is often better — provided you use it two or three times a page rather than in every sentence.

> **Common misconception 9.1**
>
> *A dash can replace any punctuation mark.* It cannot, and a page scattered with dashes reads as though the

<!-- page 77 -->

> writer could not decide. A dash is a deliberate interruption. If a sentence has more than one pair of them, the reader loses track of which interruption has ended.

#### 9.2 The hyphen

A **hyphen** joins. It has six uses, and the fourth is the one that changes meanings.

| **Use** | **Examples** |
| --- | --- |
| 1. Forming compound words | *father-in-law, passer-by, runner-up, self-esteem* |
| 2. Joining words used together before a noun | *a well-known artist, a two-hour meeting, a low-lying field* |
| 3. Joining a prefix to a root where needed | *co-ordinate, re-enter, ex-president, self-employed, non-* *violent* |
| 4. Preventing ambiguity | *a poor-rate collection* / *a poor rate-collection* |
| 5. Separating similar vowels or consonants | *pre-empt, co-operate, re-elect, Ross-shire, shell-like* |
| 6. Fractions and multipliers | *two-thirds, three-quarters, hundred-fold, twenty-five* |

*Table 9.3 — Six uses of the hyphen.*

##### 9.2.1 The attributive compound

This is the use that examinations test most. When two or more words act together as one adjective **before** a noun, they take a hyphen. When the same words come **after** the noun, they usually do not.

| **Before the noun — hyphen** | **After the noun — no hyphen** |
| --- | --- |
| a well-known artist | the artist is well known |
| a two-hour meeting | the meeting lasted two hours |
| a ten-year-old pupil | the pupil is ten years old |
| an out-of-date register | the register is out of date |
| a first-class result | the result was first class |
| a hard-working learner | the learner is hard working |

*Table 9.4 — Hyphenate before the noun, not after it.*

Note also that an adverb ending in *-ly* is never hyphenated to what follows: *a badly written report*, not *a badly-written report*. The *-ly* already shows that the word modifies the next one.

##### 9.2.2 Preventing ambiguity

| **Written** | **Means** |
| --- | --- |
| a poor-rate collection | a collection of the poor rate |
| a poor rate-collection | a badly done collection of rates |
| twenty-odd learners | about twenty learners |
| twenty odd learners | twenty strange learners |
| a small-business owner | the owner of a small business |
| a small business owner | a business owner of small stature |
| re-cover the chair | put a new cover on it |
| recover the chair | get it back |

*Table 9.5 — Four pairs in which the hyphen carries the meaning.*

##### 9.2.3 Breaking a word at the end of a line

When a word will not fit at the end of a line, it may be broken with a hyphen — but only at a **syllable break**: *edu-cation*, *infor-mation*, *punc-tuation*. Never break a word of one syllable, never leave one or two letters alone, and never break a name. In printed work this hyphen is not part of the word; it exists only because the line ran out.

<!-- page 78 -->

> **Exam tip 9.1**
>
> Hyphenation of compounds changes over time — *to-day* and *co-operate* were once standard and *today* and *cooperate* are now common. Examiners accept either where usage is genuinely divided, but never accept an inconsistent script. Choose a form and keep it throughout.

#### 9.3 Brackets

- **Brackets** — also called parentheses — enclose material that is added to a sentence without being part of its argument. They are the quietest of the three marks: what is inside them is offered as being of lesser importance.

| **Use** | **Example** |
| --- | --- |
| Something added but not of major importance | *The meeting (the third this term) lasted two hours.* |
| An explanation of something said earlier | *The by-law affects three sites (Kpando, Have and* *Golokwati).* |
| A cross reference | *The figures are given above (see Table 9.3).* |
| An abbreviation on first use | *the Water Resources Commission (WRC)* |
| A translation or gloss | *the odwira (a festival of purification)* |

*Table 9.6 — Five uses of brackets.*

**The sentence must survive without them.** Remove the bracket and everything inside, and what

- remains must be a complete, correct sentence. **Punctuation goes outside** when the bracketed material is part of a larger sentence: *The meeting lasted*

- *two hours (the third this term).* The full stop is outside the bracket. **A whole sentence inside brackets** keeps its own full stop inside: *(The committee has since been*

- *dissolved.)* **Do not nest brackets** inside brackets in ordinary writing; rewrite instead.

> - **Model 9.1 — One paragraph, three marks**
>
> **Unpunctuated:** *the district engineer a careful man in most things told the committee that the parts had arrived his exact words were the parts are at the port and that installation a two week job would begin in June the committee which had heard the same undertaking twice before see minutes of 14 March asked for a written timetable*
>
> **Punctuated:** *The district engineer — a careful man in most things — told the committee that the parts had arrived. His exact words were: “The parts are at the port.” Installation, a two-week job, would begin in June.*
>
> *The committee, which had heard the same undertaking twice before (see minutes of 14 March), asked for a written timetable.*
>
> **What the marks are doing:** the dashes hold the aside about the engineer at arm's length and give it a slight edge; the colon introduces the quoted words; *two-week* is hyphenated because it stands before the noun; the commas around *a two-week job* keep the aside close and neutral; *which had heard…* is a non-defining clause and takes commas; and the cross reference sits in brackets because it is administrative rather than part of the argument.

#### 10.1 Working out meaning from context

In a complex text the clue is often not in the same sentence. Read the sentence, then the one before and the one after, and ask what the word must be doing for the passage to make sense.

| **Clue** | **Signalled by** | **Example** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Definition | *is, means, that is*, commas, dashes | *Siltation — the settling of soil in a* *channel — has raised the bed.* |
| Example | *such as, for instance* | *Perennial crops such as cocoa and* *cashew are not replanted yearly.* |
| Contrast | *but, unlike, whereas, however* | *He was taciturn, unlike his voluble* *brother.* |
| Cause and effect | *because, so, therefore* | *The topsoil was gone, so yields* *declined.* |
| Restatement | *in other words, that is to say* | *The scheme is moribund; in other* *words, it has stopped working.* |
| General sense of the passage | no signal at all | the commonest case in difficult texts |

#### Engage

A paragraph is a machine for changing somebody's mind slightly. Where you put the topic sentence decides how the machine works: at the front, the reader knows at once what they are being asked to accept; at the end, they arrive at it having already agreed to the parts. Skilled writers use both, and choose. Most learners use the first arrangement all their lives without knowing there is a choice to be made.

#### 11.1 Joining clauses: four sentence types

A **clause** has a subject and a verb. An **independent** clause could stand alone; a **dependent** one could not. How you join them gives you four sentence types, and a paragraph made entirely of one type is tiring to read whichever type it is.

| **Type** | **Structure** | **Example** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Simple | one independent clause | *The pump failed in March.* |
| Compound | two independent clauses joined by *and, but, or, nor, for, so, yet* | *The pump failed in March, and the* *queue moved to the borehole.* |
| Complex | one independent + at least one dependent | *When the pump failed in March, the* *queue moved to the borehole.* |
| Compound-complex | two independent + at least one dependent | *When the pump failed, the queue* *moved to the borehole, and the walk* *became longer.* |

#### 11.2 Building a paragraph: major and minor support

A developed paragraph is not a list of sentences about a subject. It has a **topic sentence**, then **major supporting sentences** that directly support it, then **minor supporting sentences** that support those. Marking a paragraph's levels is the fastest way to see whether it is properly built.

> **Model 11.1 — A paragraph marked by level**
>
> **T (topic):** *The greatest obstacle to school attendance in this district is not fees but water.*
>
> **A (major):** *Learners in three of the seven communities fetch water before school.*
>
> **a (minor):** *In Damanko the nearest working borehole is 1.4 kilometres from the last house.*
>
> **a (minor):** *A survey of two Form 2 classes found thirty-one of forty-eight fetching at least twice a week.*
>
> **B (major):** *The effect falls unevenly, and it falls on girls.*
>
> **b (minor):** *Of those thirty-one, twenty-six were girls.*
>
> **b (minor):** *Boys in the same classes reported doing the work only when a sister was ill.*
>
> **C (major):** *Fees, by contrast, were remitted three years ago.*
>
> **c (minor):** *No learner in the survey named fees as a reason for lateness.*
>
> **Conclusion:** *Any policy that begins with the classroom is beginning an hour too late.*
>
> **Why the marking helps:** three major supports, each with evidence of its own; the paragraph cannot be

<!-- page 93 -->

> accused of being one claim repeated. A paragraph in which every sentence is a major support and none is minor is a list of claims; one in which every sentence is minor has lost its argument in detail.

##### 11.2.1 Four ways of developing support

| **Method** | **What it does** | **Signal** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Explanation | says how or why the claim holds | *This is because…* |
| Elaboration | takes the claim further | *More than that…* |
| Definition | fixes the meaning of a term in the claim | *By 'attendance' I mean…* |
| Example | gives an instance | *In Damanko, for example…* |
| Evidence | gives figures or testimony | *A survey of two classes found…* |

| Sequence in time | next, soon, after, then, later, suddenly, afterwards, meanwhile |
| Conclusion | in short, to sum up, in conclusion, on the whole |

*Table 11.5 — Eight groups of transitional devices.*

Three cautions. A transitional device must state a relation that is really there — *however* between two sentences that do not contrast is worse than no device at all. Overuse makes prose clank: one device every three or four sentences is plenty, because cohesion also comes from pronouns, repetition, synonyms and repeated structure. And *however*, *therefore* and *nevertheless* are adverbs, not conjunctions: they need a full stop or a semi-colon before them, never a comma alone.

#### 11.4 Where the topic sentence goes

Most paragraphs open with the topic sentence. That is the **deductive** arrangement, and it is right for most informative writing. Two other designs exist and are worth having.

| **Design** | **Topic sentence** | **Suits** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Deductive | first | reports, explanations, examinations — the reader knows at once |
| Mixed | in the middle | a paragraph that turns: evidence, then the claim, then consequences |
| Periodic | last | argument and persuasion: the reader agrees to the parts before the whole |

*Table 11.6 — Three paragraph designs.*

> **Model 11.2 — The same material in three designs**
>
> **Deductive:** *The greatest obstacle to attendance here is water, not fees. Learners in three communities fetch*

<!-- page 94 -->

> *before school; twenty-six of the thirty-one affected are girls; and fees were remitted three years ago.*
>

>
> **Which to use:** the deductive version is clearest for a reader in a hurry, and it is what an examination usually wants. The mixed version suits a paragraph that turns from one subject to another.

#### Chapter summary

- Simple, compound, complex and compound-complex are the four sentence types, and a paragraph made entirely of one is tiring to read.

- Coordinating conjunctions join equals; subordinating conjunctions attach a dependent clause and name the relation.

- *Although* and *but* each do the whole job, so using both is an error, as is *because… so*.

- A defining relative clause takes no commas and a non-defining one does, and the difference changes who is being talked about.

- A developed paragraph has a topic sentence, major supports for it, and minor supports for those.

- Support is developed by explanation, elaboration, definition, example and evidence.

- A transitional device must state a relation that is really there, and one every three or four sentences is plenty.

- *However* and *therefore* are adverbs and need a full stop or semi-colon before them, never a comma alone.

- The topic sentence may stand first (deductive), in the middle (mixed) or at the end (periodic).

<!-- page 96 -->

- Deductive is clearest and best for examinations; periodic is most persuasive for a reader who begins by disagreeing.

#### Engage

Four kinds of writing, four different demands. A description must make somebody see. A narrative must move through time without the reader noticing the machinery. An argument must survive somebody who disagrees. An explanation must be true whether the reader likes it or not. Adults are paid to do all four, and the commonest failure in each is the same: writing everything that comes to mind instead of choosing.

#### 12.1 Effective description

Weak description accumulates; strong description **selects**. Three well-chosen details beat twelve, because twelve give the reader nothing to hold and no reason to prefer one to another.

| **Describing** | **Accumulated** | **Selected** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A character | tall, dark, kind, hard-working, honest, respected | *He greeted everybody in the* *compound by name, including the* *children of the tenants.* |
| A setting | big, busy, noisy, hot, crowded, dusty | *By ten the shade had gone from the* *whole market except one strip against* *the mosque wall, and everything that* *could move had moved into it.* |
| A mood | sad, gloomy, quiet, tense, unhappy | *Nobody had turned the radio off, and* *nobody was listening to it.* |

*Table 12.1 — Accumulation against selection.*

##### 12.1.1 Sentence structure for effect

| **Device** | **What it does** | **Example** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Frontloading | puts the strongest element first, before the subject | *Torn, silted and eleven years* *unrepaired, the drain ran nowhere.* |
| Listing | accumulates deliberately, to suggest quantity or exhaustion | *She swept, she carried, she fetched* *water, she watched, she said nothing.* |
| Climactic sentence | builds through the sentence to its strongest word at the end | *They had lost the fence, the seedlings,* *two seasons of work and, in the end,* *the argument.* |
| Short sentence after long | lands a point with force | *…and the committee resolved to* *appoint a sub-committee. It never met.* |

*Table 12.2 — Four structures for effect.*

**Multiple adjectives** are permitted in description and even wanted — but in the accepted order, and rarely more than three before a noun. Beyond three, move some after the verb, or use frontloading: *Narrow, unlit and smelling of paraffin, the corridor…*

> **Model 12.1 — Describing a character**

<!-- page 100 -->

> **Weak:** *My uncle is a very kind and hard-working man. He is tall and dark and everybody in our town respects him. He has been a tailor for thirty years and he is very good at his work. He always helps people.*

> **Strong:** *My uncle has been a tailor for thirty years, and in that time he has never once delivered a garment on the day he promised it. He delivers it the day before. The reader concludes *kind* without being told, which is the whole art. #### 12.2 Manipulating time in a narrative

A story does not have to be told in the order the events happened. Four techniques give a writer control over time, and each answers a different problem.

| **Technique** | **What it does** | **The problem it solves** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Backstory | supplies what happened before the story opened | the reader needs to know something to understand the present |
| Flashback | narrates an earlier scene in full, out of an earlier event must be *felt*, not order | merely reported |
| Flash-forward | shows a later moment early | creates suspense: the reader knows where it ends but not how |
| Foreshadowing | hints at what is coming | makes the ending feel inevitable rather than convenient |

*Table 12.3 — Four ways of handling time.*

**Signal the shift.** *Two years before this, …* or a change of tense into the past perfect: *She had come to*

- *the school in the year the road was cut.* Then return clearly. **Keep flashbacks short and few.** One long flashback in a school composition is enough; three and the

- reader loses the present. **Foreshadow with objects and habits**, not with warnings. A locked drawer mentioned twice in passing

- does more than *little did I know what would happen*. **Never foreshadow what does not happen.** A hint the story does not honour is a broken promise.

> - **Model 12.2 — Three openings on the same story**
>
> **Chronological:** *In January the school appointed a new games master.*
>
> **With backstory:** *In January the school appointed a new games master. The last one had left in October, in circumstances nobody explained to us, and for three months the field had grown back to the touchline.*
>
> **Flash-forward:** *By the time the district competition came round in June, our school had a trophy, two suspensions and a headmaster who would not speak to the games master. In January, when he was appointed, nobody had expected any of the three.*

> **Which to use:** the flash-forward is the most gripping opening and the hardest to sustain, because you must now deliver all three of the things you have promised. Do not promise more than your story can pay.

##### 12.2.1 Free writing

**Free writing** is writing continuously for a fixed time without stopping to correct, cross out or judge. It is not the composition; it is how you find out what the composition is about. Set ten minutes, keep the pen moving even when you are writing *I do not know what to say*, and afterwards underline the two or three sentences that surprised you. Those are your material.

#### 12.3 The argumentative essay

An **argumentative essay** states a position and defends it. It differs from a discussion essay: you are not obliged to be neutral, but you are obliged to be fair.

| **Part** | **What it does** |
| --- | --- |
| Introduction | names the issue, states your position in one sentence |
| Argument 1 | statement, explanation, evidence, link |
| Argument 2 | the same shape |
| Argument 3 | the same shape |
| Counter-argument | the strongest case against you, stated fairly, then answered |
| Conclusion | restates the position and what follows from it — no new argument |

*Table 12.4 — The shape of an argumentative essay.*

##### 12.3.1 Hedging: claiming exactly what you can support

**Hedging** is qualifying a claim to the strength your evidence supports. It is not weakness; it is accuracy, and examiners reward it. Modals and mental verbs do the work.

| **Claim** | **Strength** | **When to use it** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sachet water *causes* the litter problem. | absolute | only with strong evidence |
| Sachet water is *the largest single* contributor. | strong, measurable | when you have a count |
| Sachet water *is likely to be* a major contributor. | hedged | reasonable inference |
| Sachet water *may* contribute. | weak | when you have little |
| *I would argue that* sachet water is the signalled as a view main problem. |  | for a position you defend |
| *It seems clear that* the litter has increased. | mental verb, moderate | for an observation |

*Table 12.5 — Six strengths of claim.*

> **Common misconception 12.1**
>
> *A strong argument uses strong words.* An argument is as strong as its evidence, and a claim pitched above its evidence is the easiest thing in the world to knock down. *All traders throw rubbish in the drain* is refuted by one trader who does not. *Most of the rubbish in the drain comes from the market* can be defended.

##### 12.3.2 The rejoinder

A **rejoinder** is a written reply to something published — an article, a letter, a report. It has a fixed form, and a rejoinder that omits the reference is unpublishable, because no reader can tell what is being answered.

| **Element** | **Detail** |
| --- | --- |
| Your address | top right, as in a formal letter |
| The date | below your address |
| The recipient's address | the editor, the publication, left |
| Salutation | *Dear Sir,* or *Dear Madam,* |
| Reference | underlined: *RE: “A LAW WORTH HAVING, BADLY MADE”,* *THE DISTRICT HERALD, 4 MAY* |
| Body | what you are replying to · what is wrong or missing · your evidence · what you ask |
| Subscription and name | *Yours faithfully*, signature, printed name, and your standing in the matter |

*Table 12.6 — The form of a rejoinder.*

The body of a rejoinder is short — three paragraphs is usual. Correct one thing well rather than five things briefly, be courteous about the writer and firm about the error, and say what you are asking for: a correction, a right of reply, or simply that readers should know a fact the original omitted.

#### 12.4 Informative and explanatory writing

An **informative text** tells the reader how or why something is as it is. Its virtue is not force but accuracy, and its language is deliberately plain.

**Describe the situation vividly but exactly.** Vivid and exact are not opposites.

- **Explain how the problem or process occurs**, step by step, in order.

- **Show what needs to be done**, and then **how**, and then **why it will work**.

- **Use objective language**: figures where you have them, attribution where you do not.

- **Organise logically** — chronological for a process, cause and effect for an explanation, problem and

- solution for a proposal.

##### 12.4.1 Writing without bias

A report must be free of judgements that cannot be measured or proved, and free of the writer's own preferences dressed as findings.

| **Biased** | **Objective** |
| --- | --- |
| The market women are careless about rubbish. | Sixty per cent of the waste sampled came from the market area. |
| The lazy contractor abandoned the work. | Work stopped on 4 March and had not resumed by 30 June. |
| A typical government project. | The project is the third of five to miss its completion date. |
| Everybody knows the water is unsafe. | Two of the four samples exceeded the permitted count. |
| The girls are less interested in science. | Of the twenty-two learners who chose the science club, six were girls. |
| The village is backward. | The village has no electricity connection and one borehole. |

*Table 12.7 — Six statements made objective.*

Notice what happens in the left column: each sentence judges a group by a standard nobody has stated. The right column reports what can be counted and leaves the reader to judge — which is not only fairer but harder to argue with.

#### 13.1 The formal letter at Basic 9

The eight parts are unchanged from Basic 8: sender's address, date, recipient's address, salutation, heading, body, subscription, signature and name. What is added at this level is **structure inside the body** — a long letter is easier to answer if it is divided.

| **Device** | **When to use it** |
| --- | --- |
| Section headings | when the letter covers more than one matter |
| Numbered paragraphs | when you expect a reply point by point |
| Bullets | for a list of items, dates or requirements |
| A summary paragraph | at the end of a long letter: *In summary, I am asking for* *three things.* |
| An enclosure line | *Enc: two receipts*, so nothing is lost |

*Table 13.1 — Structuring a long formal letter.*

**Formal language throughout.** No contractions (*do not*, not *don't*), no abbreviations except standard

- ones, no slang, no phrasal verbs where a single word exists — *investigate*, not *look into*. **One purpose per letter.** Two unrelated matters in one letter halves the chance of either being dealt

- with. **Say what you want, and by when.** A letter that does not ask for anything will be filed.

- **Proofread it twice**: once for sense, once for tone. A letter written in anger reads as anger, and anger is

- easy to dismiss.

#### 13.3 Agenda and minutes

An **agenda** is what a meeting intends to do; **minutes** are the record of what it did. Between them they decide what a meeting is remembered as having agreed, which is why the person who writes the minutes has more influence than anybody realises.

| **Agenda item** | **Purpose** |
| --- | --- |
| 1. Opening | prayer, welcome, or call to order |
| 2. Apologies for absence | who is absent and has sent word |
| 3. Minutes of the previous meeting | read, corrected and adopted |
| 4. Matters arising | items following from those minutes |
| 5–7. Substantive items | the business of this meeting, most important first |
| 8. Any other business | matters not on the agenda, taken briefly |
| 9. Date of next meeting | fixed before anybody leaves |
| 10. Closing | adjournment |

*Table 13.3 — A standard agenda.*

##### 13.3.1 Writing minutes

**Record decisions, not conversation.** Not who said what, unless a member asks for their objection to be

- **No adjectives.** *A lively discussion followed* tells a future reader nothing. Record what was decided.

#### 13.5 Articles: reports, letters to the editor, case studies

##### 13.5.1 The letter to the editor

This is the most publishable thing a school learner writes, and it succeeds by being short. Three paragraphs: what you are responding to, what you have to say that nobody else has said, and what you are asking for. Sign it with your name, your school and your town — editors do not print anonymous letters.

##### 13.5.2 The case study

A **case study** takes one instance — a household, a trader, a school, a patient — and examines it closely to show something general. It is the most persuasive form available to a school writer, because a reader who has argued with your statistics all afternoon cannot argue with a person.

| **Part** | **Contains** |
| --- | --- |
| The subject | who or what, and why this case was chosen |
| The background | how the situation arose |
| The evidence | what you observed, measured or were told, with dates |
| The wider point | what this case shows about the general issue |
| The limits | what one case cannot show |

*Table 13.5 — The five parts of a case study.*

The last row is what separates a case study from an anecdote. One household is not a district, and saying so in your own writing protects it: *This is one household of the eighteen in the community, and it was the household most affected.*

#### 13.6 Speeches

The structure is the one you learnt in Basic 8: tell them what you are going to tell them, tell them, tell them what you told them. At Basic 9 the addition is **rhetoric** — the devices that make spoken language carry.

| **Device** | **What it does** | **Example** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Emotive adjectives | carry feeling directly | *wonderful, magnificent, disgraceful,* *shocking, outrageous* |
| Tripling | three items land better than two or four | *It is your country, it is my country, it is* *our future.* |
| Repetition | fixes a phrase in the memory | *Not next year. Not next term. Now.* |
| Parallel structure | repeats a pattern so the ear expects it | *We asked in March; we asked in April;* *we are asking today.* |
| Powerful imagery | gives the audience a picture to keep | *I have a dream…* |
| Inclusive language | joins speaker and audience | *We all know…*, *you and I agree…* |
| Rhetorical question | makes the audience answer silently | *How many more terms should we* *wait?* |
| The short sentence | lands after a long one | *That is not a plan. That is a hope.* |

*Table 13.6 — Eight devices for a speech.*

Two warnings. Emotive adjectives are the easiest device and the fastest to wear out: one *disgraceful* in a speech is powerful, four is a tantrum. And every device must serve a point; a speech that is all rhetoric and no argument is remembered as noise, and the audience knows within a minute which kind it is hearing.

> **Model 13.3 — Closing of a speech, plain and rhetorical**

<!-- page 112 -->

> **Plain:** *So the school has lost about eleven days this term to fetching water. Four hundred cedis a month would end it. Another school has done it. I ask the Assembly to consider it.*
>
> **Rhetorical:** *Eleven days. Eleven days of this one term, carried up a hill in buckets by girls who should have been in a classroom. We asked in March; we asked in April; we are asking today. It is not a large thing that we are asking — four hundred cedis a month, the price of what this Assembly spends on fuel in an afternoon. A school forty kilometres from here decided it was worth it two years ago, and their girls are in class this morning. Ours are on the hill. Madam Chairperson, that is the whole of my speech.*
>
> **What was added:** tripling and parallel structure in *we asked… we asked… we are asking*; repetition of *eleven days*; a comparison that makes the sum feel small; a picture the audience can see; and a short closing sentence. Note that not one figure has changed. Rhetoric arranges evidence; it does not replace it.

#### Engage

*What are the different brands of sachet water sold in this town, and how do their prices compare?* That is a research question, and you could answer it properly by Saturday. *Is water important?* is not a research question at all, and you could work on it for a year and know nothing at the end. Almost everything that makes a school project succeed or fail is decided before any data is collected, in the sentence you write at the top of the page.

#### 14.1 The focused question

A **focused question** is narrow, answerable and worth answering. Test any question against four criteria before you go any further.

**Can it be answered with evidence?** *Should people drink sachet water?* is a matter of judgement; *how*

- *much does sachet water cost per litre compared with tap water?* is a matter of fact. **Is it narrow enough for the time you have?** One town, one market, one week.

- **Do you know what data would answer it?** If you cannot name the data, the question is not ready.

- **Is it worth answering?** Would anybody act differently if they knew?

| **Too broad** | **Focused** |
| --- | --- |
| Is water important? | How far do learners in this school walk for water before school? |
| Are cocoa products good? | What cocoa products are sold in this town, by whom, and at what price? |

#### 14.2 Choosing a method

| **Method** | **Good for** | **Weakness** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Observation and counting | what is actually there: prices, brands, traffic, waste | shows what, never why |
| Survey | what many people say or believe | wording changes the answer; people say what sounds right |
| Interview | why, and how somebody sees it | few respondents; hard to compare |
| Documents and records | the past, and figures you cannot gather yourself | records may be incomplete or out of date |
| Digital sources | background, definitions, national figures | no editor; verify before use |

*Table 14.2 — Five methods.*

Most good school projects use two methods: something counted, and somebody asked. The count gives you a fact; the interview gives you the reason. Either alone is thin.

#### 14.4 References and plagiarism

**Plagiarism** is presenting another person's words or ideas as your own. It is not a school rule invented to be irritating; in adult life it ends careers. The remedy is simple and takes seconds: say where you got it.

| **What you did** | **What you must do** |
| --- | --- |
| Copied a sentence | put it in quotation marks and name the source |
| Put an idea in your own words | name the source — paraphrase still needs a citation |
| Used a figure or a table | give the source beneath it |
| Used a photograph or a map | name who made it |
| Had help from a person | acknowledge them |
| Used your own earlier work | say so |

*Table 14.5 — Six cases, and what each requires.*

A **reference** must contain enough for another person to find the same source. Whatever style you use, be consistent throughout a piece of work.

#### 14.5 Presenting findings

| **Section** | **Contains** |
| --- | --- |
| Title | the question, or a short statement of the subject |
| The question | one sentence, exactly as investigated |
| Method | what you did, whom you asked, when, and how many |
| Findings | what the data shows — figures, tables, charts; no opinion |

#### Engage

Give two writers the same character — a ferryman, a headteacher, a trader — and they will hand you two different people, not because they invented different facts but because they gave them different sentences. Language is not the clothing of a character; it is the character. This chapter is about how that is done, and about the ideas that survive when the particular people are taken away.

#### 15.1 Conveying character through language

A writer has four instruments, and the first is the weakest.

| **Instrument** | **Example** | **Strength** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Appearance | *a heavy woman in a faded cloth* | useful, but only a surface |
| What the character does | *She counted the change twice and* *gave back a pesewa too many* | strong: action is evidence |
| What the character says, and how | *“I have two years of the four.”* | strongest: the reader hears it directly |
| What others say and do about them | *Nobody at the station would take her* *fare* | strong: it shows effect on the world |

*Table 15.1 — Four instruments of characterisation.*

**Direct characterisation** tells: *she was generous*. **Indirect characterisation** shows: *she gave back a pesewa too many*. Mature writing is mostly indirect, and where a writer does tell you outright, it is usually worth asking whether the story then proves it or quietly contradicts it.

##### 15.1.1 Idiolect: giving each character their own sentences

A character's **idiolect** is the particular way that person speaks — sentence length, vocabulary, what they will and will not say. Three characters who speak identically are one character with three names.

| **Character** | **Their sentences** | **What it reveals** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The engineer | long, qualified, passive: *the parts are* *understood to have arrived* | protects himself; speaks for an office |
| The chairman | short, plain, concrete: *I will believe it* *when I hear it running* | a lifetime of promises heard |
| The Form 3 prefect | questions, then facts: *May I ask what* *date? The last one was March.* | polite, prepared, harder to dismiss |

*Table 15.2 — Three speakers, three idiolects.*

#### 15.3 The sequence of events across text types

| **Text type** | **How events are ordered** | **Why** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Descriptive writing | no sequence; organised by space or by nothing happens; the reader is placed sense |  |
| Autobiography | chronological, but selected by what the writer now understands | the writer knows how it ended |
| Biography | chronological, with evidence and dates | the reader must be able to check |
| Narrative fiction | any order the writer chooses | effect, not record |
| Play script | scene by scene, in stage time | what cannot be staged must be reported |
| Film and media | shot by shot; cuts do the work of connectives | the eye supplies what is not shown |

**Autobiography differs from biography** in more than the writer. An autobiographer knows the ending

- and shapes the beginning towards it; a biographer must show the evidence for each stage.

**A play cannot narrate.** What happens between scenes must be reported by a character, which is why

- dramatists invent messengers, letters and returning travellers. **Film cuts.** A man in a boat; a bridge; the empty landing. No word says *afterwards*, and every viewer

- supplies it.

In Passage 15.1 the events are given in this order: thirty-one years of ferrying; his manner of speaking; Auntie Mansa's nine years; his sons leaving; the bridge opening in March; the present Thursdays. ##### 15.4.1 The ballad

A **ballad** tells a story in four-line stanzas, usually in **common metre** — lines of eight and six syllables — rhyming *abcb*. It is the oldest narrative form in English and it is meant to be heard.

A **cinquain** has five lines of two, four, six, eight and two syllables. Its shape does the work: it opens, widens, and shuts in two syllables — which is why the last line should turn rather than summarise.

> **Common misconception 15.1**
>
> *Imagery means describing things beautifully.* Imagery means making the reader perceive — and the perception should serve the meaning. *The river paid no heed* is not decoration; it is the entire argument of the ballad, which is that the world does not notice what it costs us. If you can remove an image and lose nothing but prettiness, remove it.

#### 15.6 Common themes

#### Chapter summary

- A writer conveys character through appearance, action, speech and the words of others — and appearance is the weakest of the four.

- Indirect characterisation shows; direct characterisation tells, and mature writing is mostly indirect.

<!-- page 131 -->

- A character's idiolect is their own way of speaking; three identical voices are one character with three names.

- Dialogue shows what characters will say to one another; a monologue shows what one of them will not.

- Autobiography is shaped by an ending the writer already knows; biography must show its evidence.

- A play cannot narrate, so what happens off stage must be reported by a character; film uses cuts where prose uses connectives.

- A ballad tells a story in stanzas of eight and six syllables rhyming abcb; a cinquain runs 2-4-6-8-2; a haiku 5-7- 5.

- Identify a poetic form by counting, not by guessing from the subject.

- Imagery makes the reader perceive, and an image that could be removed without loss of meaning should be removed.

- A theme is a sentence about life, not a word, and it is not a moral: a text need not approve of what it shows.
