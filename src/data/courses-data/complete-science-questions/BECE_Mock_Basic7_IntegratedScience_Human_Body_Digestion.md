### EXAMINER NOTES

- The oesophagus and the trachea are confused by many candidates. Fix the difference once: the oesophagus carries food to the stomach, the trachea carries air to the lungs; a paper that sends food down the windpipe earns no mark.
- In Question 1(a) the small and large intestines must be told apart by their appearance: the small intestine is the long, narrow, coiled tube, while the large intestine is the shorter, wider tube that frames it.
- The three end products - glucose, amino acids, and fatty acids and glycerol - are the heart of indicator B7/JHS1.3.1.1.3. Drill them as a flow chart, not as a list, so that candidates write 'starch gives glucose' rather than three unconnected words.
- Question 3(b) separates physical from chemical digestion. Candidates who give no example from the meal should not be given the third mark, however good the definitions are.
- Question 4(b) attacks the common idea that food falls down the canal by gravity. The muscular squeezing of the canal wall is the correct answer; a class demonstration with a soft ball pushed along a rubber tube makes it plain.
- The food tests in Question 6 should be done, not only read about. Iodine on yam, Benedict's solution on a glucose drink, and the grease spot from palm oil on paper can all be managed in any Ghanaian school laboratory.
- Follow-up work: let pupils chew a piece of bread for two minutes and then test it with iodine and with Benedict's solution, as in Question 6(c). The result teaches the action of saliva better than any amount of dictation.
